1. COURSE DESCRIPTION 
 
Emerging technologies are progressively affecting the way we relate, connect, 
learn, and work. In this course you will study psychological processes associated 
with the use of digital technologies, to help understand how technology affects 
us, and to enhance interactions between humans and technologies. We will 
discuss research in relation to the use and design of a range of applications and 
devices, for instance, cell phones, social media, video games, and the Internet. 
The course will include topics such as the relation between cognitive processes 
and emotions, social identity and group behavior, and interpersonal 
relationships. The course will mainly draw on theories from cognitive and social 
psychology and will involve critical analysis and understanding of these theories 
in view of our digital world. 
 
Prerequisite knowledge 
Students are expected to be familiar (or be prepared to familiarize themselves) 
with basic knowledge of cognitive psychology and emotion theories as taught in 
the bachelor course Cognition & Emotion, and basic (social) psychological effects 
of technology on individuals and societies as taught in the bachelor courses 
Mens, Maatschappij & ICT en Informatieuitwisseling. 
 
If you are unfamiliar with (some of) these topics, we suggest reading the 
following introductory texts into these fields. Both these texts are available 
through the university library after logging in or when on campus network. 
 
• 
Cognitive psychology – Galotti, K.M. (2017) Cognitive Psychology: 
History, methods and paradigms. In Galotti, K.M. (ed.). Cognitive 
psychology in and out of the laboratory (pp.2-34), Sage Publications: 
Thousand Oaks, CA. 
• 
Social psychology - DeLamater, J.D. (2018). Introduction to social 
psychology. In DeLamater, J.D., Myers, D.J., & Collett, J. (eds.) Social 
Psychology (pp. 1-29), Routledge: New York, NY, USA. 
 
2. LEARNING GOALS 
 
After this course, a student will be able to: 
• 
Apply cognitive and social psychology theories to HCI problems, such as 
those regarding memory, attention, cognitive processing and cognitive 
load, human decision making, emotions, engagement and motivation, 
social identity, interpersonal communication, social influence, and group 
dynamics. 
• 
Evaluate applications in respect to cognitive and social psychology 
theories. 
• 
Critically review research in human-robot interaction, affective computing, 
and games in terms of these theories.

3. CLASSROOM POLICIES 
 
3.2 CLASSROOM ENVIRONMENT 
 
Our goal is to create a learning environment in which everyone receives a sense 
of authentic belonging from the group and, at the same time, is valued for their 
unique characteristics as an individual. 
 
Meaningful and constructive dialogue is encouraged in this class. This requires a 
degree of mutual respect, willingness to listen, and tolerance of opposing points 
of view. Classroom discussion should be civilized and respectful to everyone and 
relevant to the topic we are discussing. Everyone is entitled to their opinion. 
Classroom discussion is meant to allow us to hear a variety of viewpoints. This 
can only happen if we respect each other and our differences. One’s words and 
use of language should be temperate and within acceptable bounds of civility 
and decency. Our use of language should be respectful of other persons or 
groups. You need not represent any group, only yourself, though you may 
choose to represent a group if you wish.  
 
Behavior in this class will be in adherence to the University Code of Conduct. If 
you feel uncomfortable about any aspect of the class environment, please do not 
hesitate to discuss it with the course coordinator. 
 
3.2 USING AI GENERATED TOOLS 
 
AI programs such as ChatGPT, Dall-E, and GitHub Copilot are one of the many 
tools that use artificial intelligence to generate certain outputs. Although good 
possibility that using tools such as these are going to become an important skill 
for careers in the not distant future, there is still an ongoing (ethical) debate of 
when using these tools may be acceptable for three important reasons: 
• 
Work created by AI tools may not be considered original work, but instead 
be a form of automated plagiarism. The provided output is derived from 
previously created texts or pictures from other sources that the models 
were trained on without properly giving credit to these sources. 
• 
AI models have built-in biases as they are trained on limited underlying 
sources and reproduce —rather than challenge— errors in the sources. 
• 
AI tools have limitations as they lack critical thinking to evaluate and 
reflect on criteria and lack abductive reasoning to make judgments with 
incomplete information at hand. 
 
Academic integrity is a core principle at our university and all students should 
uphold this principle. Responsible use of AI-based tools in completing 
coursework or assessments must be done in accordance with the following: 
1. You must clearly identify the use of AI-based tools in your work. Any work 
that utilizes AI-based tools must be clearly marked as such, including the 
specific tool(s) used. For example, if you use ChatGPT-3, you must cite 
"ChatGPT-3. (YYYY, Month DD of query). "Text of your query." Generated 
using OpenAI. https://chat.openai.com/"

4. ASSESSMENT AND GRADING 
 
4.1 WRITTEN EXAM – SCORE: 50% 
 
The written exam will consist of both open and multiple-choice questions based 
on the weekly reading materials and content of the lectures. 
 
4.2 RESEARCH PROPOSAL – SCORE: 25% 
 
Students will write a research proposal in teams of three students. The proposal 
should cover a psychology topic related to the course. More details about the 
specific requirements and assessment criteria can be found in section 5.1. 
 
4.3 INTERACTIVE CLASS SESSION – SCORE: 25% 
 
In groups of four to five, students will prepare and lead an interactive class 
session on a focus area related to one of the weekly topics. More details about 
the specific requirements and assessment criteria can be found in section 5.2. 
 
4.4 GRADING 
 
Your final grade will be based on the theoretical (50%) and practical (50%) part 
of the course. The individual grade for each part needs to be at least 5.5 to pass 
the course. 
 
The theoretical part consists of the final exam. Your grade of the exam will count 
towards 50% of your final grade and needs to be at least 5.5 to pass the course. 
 
The practical part consists of two assignments: (1) the interactive class session 
and (2) the research proposal. Each will count for 25% towards your final grade 
and each assignment needs to be at least 4.5 to pass the course. 
 
Calculation of final grade = 0.5 * final exam + 0.25 * research proposal + 0.25 
* interactive class session. However, is the weighted average of either the 
theoretical or practical part below 5.5 OR any of the individual grades below 4.5, 
than your final grade of the course will be a maximum of 5.0. 
 
Note that the grade you received is based on your work in relation to the best 
possible conception, as well as the full spectrum of submitted work, and is not a 
simple matter of "starting at 10 and deducting n points for every negative 
comment".

4.5 RETAKE 
 
In case of a failing grade for the practical part of the course (the two 
assignments), you may qualify for a repair assignment when your initial grade 
for each course part (i.e., theoretical and practical) are at least a 4.0 and you 
meet the best-efforts obligation. The best-efforts obligation means that a 
student has participated in all course assessments and has met all its deadlines. 
The maximum grade during a retake attempt of the practical part of the course 
is a 6.0. Please contact the course coordinator if you would like to make use of 
this opportunity. 
 
In case of a failing grade for the theoretical part of the course (the written 
exam), you can enroll for the retake exam. 
 
5. ASSIGNMENTS 
 
5.1 RESEARCH PROPOSAL 
 
You work in teams of two to three students and together you write one research 
proposal. 
 
The aim of the assignment is to help you: 
• 
Develop your skills in identifying research topics, formulate research 
questions and design a comprehensive research study. 
• 
Learn how to conduct a review of the literature to ensure a research 
problem has not already been answered or has been answered 
insufficiently.  
• 
Practice identifying the logical steps that must be taken to accomplish 
one's research goals. 
• 
Critically review, examine, and consider the use of different methods 
for gathering and analyzing data related to the research problem.  
• 
Practice your writing skills.  
• 
See yourself as an active participant in the process of doing research. 
 
General description 
The course Advanced Cognitive & Social Psychology covers several topics related 
to cognitive and social psychology, and ICT. You either select one of the 
cognitive psychology topics or one of the social psychology topics, that you think 
hold research questions that require further investigation. You will define a 
research question and develop a proposal to explore and answer the question, 
using scientific methods. In this assignment, you can keep a master thesis 
research project in mind, that generally requires 9 months to complete.  
 
Your proposal should contain all the key elements involved in designing a 
research study. The text should include sufficient information to allow readers to 
assess the validity and usefulness of your proposed study. Make sure that you 
understand the implications of these demands.

Regardless of the research problem you are investigating and the methodology 
you choose, all research proposals must address the following topics: 
1. 
Research question. What do you plan to accomplish? Be clear and 
succinct in defining the research problem and what it is you are 
proposing to research. Why do you want to do it? 
2. 
Theoretical framework based on literature review. Conduct a review of 
the literature and provide convincing evidence that it is a topic worthy 
of study. Explain relevant concepts, definitions, models, and theories. 
What questions have other researchers asked; what methods have 
they used; what is your understanding of their findings? Explain why 
your proposal is relevant for the domain.  
3. 
Research design and methods. How are you going to do it? The 
implementation of a research problem into a doable study is a complex 
task. Always keep in mind the type of information you need to answer 
your research question. Consider methods and techniques used in 
studies from your literature review, or alternative methods. Describe 
how you expect to analyze the results.  
4. 
Preliminary suppositions and implications. Explain how the anticipated 
results will impact future scholarly research, theory, or practice; so 
theoretical, methodological, or substantial/practical significance. 
5. 
Conclusion. A summary of the entire study in about two paragraphs, 
highlighting why the research problem is worth investigating, why your 
research study is unique, and how it should advance existing 
knowledge. 
6. 
References. References to literature and other sources, in APA style. 
We expect about 10 references to publications of good scientific 
quality.

5.2 INTERACTIVE CLASS SESSION 
 
You work in teams of four to five students and together you will prepare one 
interactive session. 
 
The aim of the assignment is to help you: 
• 
Develop your critical thinking techniques to form academically 
supported opinions. 
• 
Understand and retain key issues in the materials presented in class. 
• 
Investigate theoretical constructs more deeply. 
• 
Organize and present class material to others in an interesting and 
informative way. 
• 
Develop and demonstrate communication skills. 
 
General description 
The course Advanced Cognitive & Social Psychology covers seven topics related 
to cognitive and/or social psychology, and ICT. Each week will address one of 
these topics for which student teams will prepare an interactive class activity. 
 
Form a group of four students. Pick one of the seven topics (see Schedule for 
weekly topics) for which you prepare: 
1. 
a short presentation (length: max. 10 minutes) 
2. 
an interactive class session (length: ±20 minutes) 
 
We aim to have two student groups per week, with a maximum of three student 
groups on the same topic. With your group, you will develop expertise on a 
specific focus area related to that week’s topic guided by one or more research 
questions you would like to address. You can start by reading the literature 
assigned to that week, but you will have to extend your knowledge and focus by 
searching additional academic papers and scanning online news media for 
current events related to your specific focus area. The goal of this part is to 
broaden our understanding of that week’s topic by explaining your insights into 
your chosen focus area. 
 
You will prepare the session based on four academic papers: 
1. 
Foundation paper that presents basic theoretical principles (often with 
high number of citations). 
2. 
Relatively new paper with contemporary views on the theory. 
3. 
Empirical paper that tests the theoretical principles based on 
observation and measurement. 
4. 
Critical paper that questions the theoretical base of the foundation 
paper. 
 
You will write a one-pager that shortly presents your focus area based on the 
four academic papers, how it relates to that week’s topic, and which open 
research questions or issues you have planned to address in the interactive class 
session. Note that this one-pager and your presentation is not merely a 
summary of the selected papers and the views of the authors; it should be an 
engaging introduction on your focus area that addresses the importance of the 
theoretical principles and arguments for and against.

EXAMPLE SCENARIO
SCENARIO:
Liam, a mountain lover, finally convinces his friends Mark and Lisa to 
go for a trekking trip. Liam is an experienced mountaineer and 
plans the route because the others have never been trekking before. 
The day passes by smoothly, but on their way to their campsite, they 
realize they are lost. They hear something that resembles howling. 
Mark thinks that could be a wolf. He is very scared. He notices his 
heart racing, and he's suddenly feeling very warm. Lisa barely 
notices the howling, but she observes Mark's reaction, and she starts 
feeling scared as well. Liam is aware that his friends are scared. He 
is very calm instead, and he does not feel any physiological 
reaction. "Don't be scared. Sometimes owls really resemble wolves, 
but the presence of a wolf here is very unlikely. There is nothing to 
be afraid of", he says, to avoid panic and he continues hiking to the 
campsite. Mark and Lisa look at each other. They are still worried, 
but they do not feel like arguing with Liam. They simply want to set 
up camp before sunset.

a.

--- Page 14 ---
EXAMPLE SCENARIO
b. Describe how the underlying theories work in general while 
providing a bit more detail on aspects related to the 
scenario (max 2).
ANSWER FOR THEORY OF CONSTRUCTED EMOTIONS:
According to the theory of constructed emotions, how we 
interpret our emotions is not biologically prewired, rather, 
emotions emerge when our sensations (simple feelings) become 
conscious and give these sensations meaning. Such simple 
feelings are a basic summary of what is going on inside the 
body and we are then interpreting them by comparing them to 
past experiences, evaluating what is going on around us in the 
world, which leads to us responding to the situation. These 
constructions are the emerging emotions (or affect).

--- Page 15 ---
EXAMPLE SCENARIO
c.
Apply the theories to the situation.
ANSWER FOR OBEDIENCE:
Lisa and Mark's decision to follow Liam in continuing their hike to 
camp captures a behavior of obedience. Liam, the authoritative 
figure due to his hiking expertise, states that the emotions are 
inappropriate, and his behavior (continuing their hike) suggests 
that it is safe to set up camp. Lisa and Mark, who have entered 
and subconsciously agreed to the social hierarchy, do not doubt 
Liam's opinion, trusting the leader of the group. Since they have 
so far followed Liam’s lead (behavior consistency), want to reach 
their campsite in time (group shared goal), and feel 
uncomfortable to speak up (respecting social hierarchy), they 
disregard any potential danger and continue hiking to camp..

Social Penetration Theory
[Altman & Taylor, 1978]
Describes the process of bonding that moves a relationship from 
superficial to more intimate and explain how information exchange (self-
disclosure) functions in the development and dissolution of interpersonal 
relationships.

Orientation Stage — Getting acquainted with “small talk” and first impressions. 
Interaction adheres to social norms.

Exploratory Affective Stage — Revealing more about themselves. Casual friendships are 
established. Most relationships stay at this level.

Affective Stage — Disclosure of personal and private matters. Comfortable enough to 
argue or criticize each other. Romantic relationships develop at this stage.

Stable Stage — Disclosure is open and comfortable. Able to predict how the other 
person will react to certain types of information.

Depenetration — One or both communicators perceive that the cost of self-disclosure 
outweighs its benefits. Withdraw from self-disclosure, thus ending the relationship.

--- Page 15 ---
Social Penetration Theory
[Altman & Taylor, 1978]
Reward-Cost Assessment

Self-disclosure results in vulnerability.

Amount of self-disclosure depends on cost-reward assessment.

Reward-cost assessments become more critical and frequent as relationships deepen 
and the risks of self-disclosure rise.
Barriers to Self-Disclosure

Person factors of influence: gender, race, religion, personality, social status, and ethnic 
background.

Can slow the rate of self-disclosure and even prevent relationships from forming.

Social 
Compensation 
Hypothesis
[McKenna & Bargh, 2000]
Introverts will primarily benefit from 
the internet.

Reduced social cues and anonymity 
compensates for a lack of social skills.

This eases self-disclosures which facilitates

--- Page 18 ---
Antecedents of 
online friendship formation 
[Peter et al., 2005]
A combination of self-disclosure and intro/extra-version.

Extraverted adolescents self-disclosed and communicated online more 
frequently, which facilitated the formation of online friendships.

Introverted adolescents were more strongly motivated to communicate 
online to compensate for lacking social skills, which increased their 
chances of making friends online.
Psychological characteristics together with motives and 
internet use patterns determine online friendship formation.

Both the rich-get-richer and the social compensation hypotheses fall 
short in explaining online friendship formation.

Online friendship formation is complex.

Social desirability
• The tendency to respond to questions in a 
way that will be seen favourably by others.
• Social desirability is a challenge for any self-
report method
Examples:
• Exaggerating the amount of physical exercise
• Inflating the amount that one has donated to charities
• Denying or not reporting frowned-upon behaviours, such as 
cheating, or lying

--- Page 15 ---
Personality differences
• Personality aspects, such as how agreeable or 
how self-confident we are can affect how we 
think of ourselves. This makes accurate 
measurement with self-report difficult.
Example: A humble person would likely think of 
themselves as less humble than a non-humble 
person. Their humility might be precisely why 
they would likely indicate that they are less 
humble than they actually are.

Low Affect Infusion Strategies (reconstructive)
(not likely to be influenced by affect)
(1) direct access to a preexisting evaluation
Direct access processing is likely when the target is known or familiar. The judge is 
not personally involved; there are no strong cognitive, affective, motivational, or 
situational forces mandating elaborate processing.
(2) motivated processing in service of a preexisting goal
Likely when there are strong motivational pressures towards a judgmental 
outcome. In these circumstances, judges are likely to engage in a selective 
information search. Motivated processing is when preferences guide judgments.

--- Page 18 ---
High Affect Infusion Strategies (constructive)
(likely to be influenced by affect)
(3) heuristic processing
When neither a prior evaluation nor a strong motivational goal help to determine 
the outcome and there is limited cognitive capacity. Heuristic processing requires 
the minimum possible effort, considering only some of the available information 
and using whatever shortcuts or simplifications are readily available.
(4) substantive processing
Substantive processing requires judges to select, learn, and interpret novel 
information about a target and relate this information to preexisting knowledge 
structures. It is more likely when the target is complex or atypical and the judge has 
adequate cognitive capacity and is motivated to be accurate.

Approach and Avoidance Motivation

outcome; trying to minimize negative affect
(e.g., studying in order 
to not fail an exam)

Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivation
Intrinsic Motivation
has been defined as:
• “in the activity”
• “the need for self-determination”
• “interest and involvement”
• “a correspondence between

Extrinsic Motivation
• Is often equated with activities

Flow Theory
Flow is often described as a pleasant and motivating state that is likely to

Self-

Individuals tend to experience more flow, intrinsic 
motivation, and well-being in situations in which they 
experience three universal human needs (that all individuals 
share): relatedness, autonomy, and competence. The 
theory suggests that individuals will seek out situations that

• This theory centers around the experiential outcomes of a situation 
and in general focuses on improving situations rather than on 
understanding individuals.

Social Penetration Theory
[Altman & Taylor, 1978]
Describes the process of bonding that moves a relationship from 
superficial to more intimate and explain how information exchange (self-
disclosure) functions in the development and dissolution of interpersonal 
relationships.

Orientation Stage — Getting acquainted with “small talk” and first impressions. 
Interaction adheres to social norms.

Exploratory Affective Stage — Revealing more about themselves. Casual friendships are 
established. Most relationships stay at this level.

Affective Stage — Disclosure of personal and private matters. Comfortable enough to 
argue or criticize each other. Romantic relationships develop at this stage.

Stable Stage — Disclosure is open and comfortable. Able to predict how the other 
person will react to certain types of information.

Depenetration — One or both communicators perceive that the cost of self-disclosure 
outweighs its benefits. Withdraw from self-disclosure, thus ending the relationship.

--- Page 15 ---
Social Penetration Theory
[Altman & Taylor, 1978]
Reward-Cost Assessment

Self-disclosure results in vulnerability.

Amount of self-disclosure depends on cost-reward assessment.

Reward-cost assessments become more critical and frequent as relationships deepen 
and the risks of self-disclosure rise.
Barriers to Self-Disclosure

Person factors of influence: gender, race, religion, personality, social status, and ethnic 
background.

Can slow the rate of self-disclosure and even prevent relationships from forming.

[Kraut et al., 2002]
Also known as Rich-get-richer
Extroverts will primarily benefit from 
the internet.

Make easier contacts.

Fully develop their already greater social

--- Page 17 ---
Social 
Compensation 
Hypothesis
[McKenna & Bargh, 2000]
Introverts will primarily benefit from 
the internet.

Reduced social cues and anonymity 
compensates for a lack of social skills.

This eases self-disclosures which facilitates 
friendship formation.

--- Page 18 ---
Antecedents of 
online friendship formation 
[Peter et al., 2005]
A combination of self-disclosure and intro/extra-version.

Extraverted adolescents self-disclosed and communicated online more 
frequently, which facilitated the formation of online friendships.

Introverted adolescents were more strongly motivated to communicate 
online to compensate for lacking social skills, which increased their 
chances of making friends online.
Psychological characteristics together with motives and 
internet use patterns determine online friendship formation.

Both the rich-get-richer and the social compensation hypotheses fall 
short in explaining online friendship formation.

Online friendship formation is complex.

Social desirability
• The tendency to respond to questions in a 
way that will be seen favourably by others.
• Social desirability is a challenge for any self-
report method
Examples:
• Exaggerating the amount of physical exercise
• Inflating the amount that one has donated to charities
• Denying or not reporting frowned-upon behaviours, such as 
cheating, or lying

--- Page 15 ---
Personality differences
• Personality aspects, such as how agreeable or 
how self-confident we are can affect how we 
think of ourselves. This makes accurate 
measurement with self-report difficult.
Example: A humble person would likely think of 
themselves as less humble than a non-humble 
person. Their humility might be precisely why 
they would likely indicate that they are less 
humble than they actually are.
