Qualitative study design (25%)

- Define a research question
- Select at least two qualitative methods
- Produce an initial protocol for each of these methods including all materials required to run the studies. Justify any decisions made (for example, provide a rationale for questions asked). 
- Run pilot studies for each
- Reflect on how it went, what kind of information did it provide, what worked well in your pilot study/what needs to be improved. No need to fully analyze the results, just focus on the usefulness of the method for producing the information you want.
- Decide which qualitative method(s) you will use in your real study. Also reflect on other methods taught in this course, and how they could or could not be used for future research related to your research question (and what additional insights they might bring).  
- Improve the protocol for the method(s) you will use in your study
- Write a report on the above (15 pages max). Submission deadline:  Friday 8 December 23:00


Qualitative study execution and data analysis plan (25%)

- Decide on sampling method, sample size and recruitment strategy for your study
- Recruit participants and run the study
- Decide how you will analyse/report/visualize the data
- Test this analysis method out on part of your data
- Improve your data analysis method where needed
- Prepare a presentation (maximum 6 minutes) on the above;  Submission deadline: Thursday 21 December 12:00; To be presented by your pair in class on Thursday 21 December. (Some pairs can present on Tuesday 20 if they wish.)  


Academic paper (30%). 
- Report your study in the form of an academic paper (including introduction, study design, study results and discussion, conclusions and limitations). Format will be decided by the scientific event program chairs. Submission deadline: 16 January 23:00. 


Organization of and participation in scientific event (20%):

- Act in various conference organization roles
- Review papers as reviewer and/or meta reviewer. Reviews submission deadline Friday 19 January 23:00. Meta reviews submission deadline Monday 22 January 20:00.  
- Present a paper orally or as poster. Submission of presentation or poster Wednesday 24 January 23:00. To be presented by you on Thursday 25 January in the event.  Whether you present orally or as a poster depends on the outcome of the review process.

ABSTRACT
Human–Computer Interaction (HCI) addresses problems of interaction design: understanding 
user needs to inform design, delivering novel designs that meet user needs, and evaluating new 
and existing designs to determine their success in meeting user needs. Qualitative methods have 
an essential role to play in this enterprise, particularly in understanding user needs and behaviours 
and evaluating situated use of technology. Qualitative methods allow HCI researchers to ask 
questions where the answers are more complex and interesting than “true” or “false,” and may also 
be unexpected. In this lecture, we draw on the analogy of making a documentary film to discuss 
important issues in qualitative HCI research: historically, films were presented as finished prod­
ucts, giving the viewer little insight into the production process; more recently, there has been a 
trend to go behind the scenes to expose some of the painstaking work that went into creating the 
final cut. Similarly, in qualitative research, the essential work behind the scenes is rarely discussed. 
There are many “how to” guides for particular methods, but few texts that start with the purpose 
of a study and then discuss the important details of how to select a suitable method, how to adapt 
it to fit the study context, or how to deal with unexpected challenges that arise. We address this 
gap by presenting a repertoire of qualitative techniques for understanding user needs, practices and 
experiences with technology for the purpose of informing design. We also discuss practical con­
siderations such as tactics for recruiting participants and ways of getting started when faced with 
a pile of interview transcripts. Our particular focus is on semi-structured qualitative studies, which 
occupy a space between ethnography and surveys—typically involving observations, interviews and 
similar methods for data gathering, and methods of analysis based on systematic coding of data. Just 
as a documentary team faces challenges that often go unreported when arranging expeditions or 
interviews and gathering and editing footage within time and budget constraints, so the qualitative 
research team faces challenges in obtaining ethical clearance, recruiting participants, analysing data, 
choosing how and what to report, etc. We present illustrative examples drawn from prior experience 
to bring to life the purpose, planning and practical considerations of doing qualitative studies for 
interaction design. We include takeaway checklists for planning, conducting, reporting and evalu­
ating semi-structured qualitative studies.

Preface
The motivation for writing this book came from several different directions. The key driver was that 
there has not been a good text that we could direct our students to that resonates with their interests 
and the problems they are addressing. There are some excellent texts from the social sciences and 
from healthcare, but they do not deal with problems of interaction design, user requirements or 
user experience when interacting with technology. Conversely, there are many excellent HCI texts 
that focus on observation, task analysis or research methods, but none that focus specifically on 
qualitative methods. We intend that this book should plug that gap.
A second motivation comes from the perennial question or challenge: what constitutes qual­
ity in qualitative HCI research? Is it even research? We should raise the quality of the discourse on 
what constitutes valid, reliable and valuable qualitative research in HCI. We should also raise the 
quality of that research, so that it becomes more valuable and has greater integrity.
We have chosen to draw on the analogy of going behind the scenes when making a docu­
mentary. Our main sources of inspiration for this have been Dom’s experience of making short films 
to communicate our research and Ann’s delight at watching “behind the scenes” footage on the 
making of wildlife documentaries (BBC, 2014). Of course, a qualitative study is expected to have a 
scientific rigour that is not expected of many documentaries, but they both share issues in gathering 
data, creating a narrative and representing some aspect of reality to inform an audience. Our focus 
on going behind the scenes means that we draw a lot on our own experiences, because we know 
what went on behind the scenes in our studies. So long as there is little tradition of reporting these 
details, that information is not accessible for other researchers’ projects, and it is difficult to be re­
flective about the work of others when you don’t have the insider knowledge. So we hope this book 
will encourage you to consider taking readers of your own research “behind the scenes”—providing 
them with useful detail and justification on what you did and why.
In this book, we are pre-supposing a good general knowledge of HCI, but less detailed 
knowledge of qualitative methods. Our primary audience is Master’s and Ph.D. students in HCI 
and related areas who are planning their individual projects. Other audiences include HCI practi­
tioners who are planning in-depth studies, or people with a background in qualitative methods but 
who are new to HCI. We hope that this book will help you design and conduct excellent qualitative 
HCI studies.
Ann Blandford, Dominic Furniss and Stephann Makri, February 2016

ABSTRACT
Human–Computer Interaction (HCI) addresses problems of interaction design: understanding 
user needs to inform design, delivering novel designs that meet user needs, and evaluating new 
and existing designs to determine their success in meeting user needs. Qualitative methods have 
an essential role to play in this enterprise, particularly in understanding user needs and behaviours 
and evaluating situated use of technology. Qualitative methods allow HCI researchers to ask 
questions where the answers are more complex and interesting than “true” or “false,” and may also 
be unexpected. In this lecture, we draw on the analogy of making a documentary film to discuss 
important issues in qualitative HCI research: historically, films were presented as finished prod­
ucts, giving the viewer little insight into the production process; more recently, there has been a 
trend to go behind the scenes to expose some of the painstaking work that went into creating the 
final cut. Similarly, in qualitative research, the essential work behind the scenes is rarely discussed. 
There are many “how to” guides for particular methods, but few texts that start with the purpose 
of a study and then discuss the important details of how to select a suitable method, how to adapt 
it to fit the study context, or how to deal with unexpected challenges that arise. We address this 
gap by presenting a repertoire of qualitative techniques for understanding user needs, practices and 
experiences with technology for the purpose of informing design. We also discuss practical con­
siderations such as tactics for recruiting participants and ways of getting started when faced with 
a pile of interview transcripts. Our particular focus is on semi-structured qualitative studies, which 
occupy a space between ethnography and surveys—typically involving observations, interviews and 
similar methods for data gathering, and methods of analysis based on systematic coding of data. Just 
as a documentary team faces challenges that often go unreported when arranging expeditions or 
interviews and gathering and editing footage within time and budget constraints, so the qualitative 
research team faces challenges in obtaining ethical clearance, recruiting participants, analysing data, 
choosing how and what to report, etc. We present illustrative examples drawn from prior experience 
to bring to life the purpose, planning and practical considerations of doing qualitative studies for 
interaction design. We include takeaway checklists for planning, conducting, reporting and evalu­
ating semi-structured qualitative studies.

Preface
The motivation for writing this book came from several different directions. The key driver was that 
there has not been a good text that we could direct our students to that resonates with their interests 
and the problems they are addressing. There are some excellent texts from the social sciences and 
from healthcare, but they do not deal with problems of interaction design, user requirements or 
user experience when interacting with technology. Conversely, there are many excellent HCI texts 
that focus on observation, task analysis or research methods, but none that focus specifically on 
qualitative methods. We intend that this book should plug that gap.
A second motivation comes from the perennial question or challenge: what constitutes qual­
ity in qualitative HCI research? Is it even research? We should raise the quality of the discourse on 
what constitutes valid, reliable and valuable qualitative research in HCI. We should also raise the 
quality of that research, so that it becomes more valuable and has greater integrity.
We have chosen to draw on the analogy of going behind the scenes when making a docu­

them with useful detail and justification on what you did and why.

3.1 方法
3.1.1 焦点小组 在本研究中，我们采用了两个半结构式焦

半结构化的形式兼顾了预先确定的问题和自发讨论的机会。
这种方法有助于收集有关取消一次性纸杯及其对校园可持续

3.1.2 创造性解决问题 在本研究中，采用了两次创造性解决
问题（CPS）会议。CPS 会议代表了一种结构化的小组头脑风
暴方法，重点是为本研究背景下确定的改动提出想法和解决
方案。与其他方法不同的是，CPS 会议优先考虑构思，而不
是收集有关学生意见、经验和行为的各种数据。因此，事实
证明，CPS 课程能在相对较短的时间内产生更多的想法。值
得注意的是，这种方法强调培养创造性和非传统的想法，这
一点对本研究尤为重要，因为本研究旨在全面了解大学生对
取消一次性纸杯的看法。
CPS 课程的适应性体现在可采用的各种方法上。为了与本研
究的目标保持一致，我们选择了 Synectics 过程方法，特别是

过在 CPS 课程中应用 Synectics 过程方法，旨在挖掘学生的创
造力，探索可能被忽视的创新解决方案。

5
讨论
5.1 焦点小组调查结果的解读
焦点小组的研究结果从多方面反映了学生对乌特勒支大学近
期政策变化的反应。图 3 直观地显示了参与者在主要专题类
别中的反应分布。值得注意的是，大多数人表达了对政策变
化的不满，强调了挑战和担忧。不过，也有相当一部分人对
政策表示支持，特别是在环境考虑方面。与传播策略有关的
改进建议也很突出，同时还讨论了政策变化带来的行为改变
。
思维导图（图 4-7）对每个类别进行了更深入的探讨，概述了
次主题和重复出现的答 复。在 "挑战与挫折 "类别中，与会者
表达了适应新政策的困难，包括健忘和实际不便。这直接影
响了 "行为改变 "类别，参与者讨论了习惯的改变，如更多地
使用可重复使用的杯子，但偶尔也会忘记。与此同时，"支持
环境可持续发展政策 "类别中的积极态度显示了挑战与对可持
续发展的总体承诺之间的复杂关系。
此外，与会者的挫折和挑战为他们的 "改进建议 "提供了背景
。有效沟通策略的必要性成为一个中心主题，这与参与者在
适应政策变化时所面临的挑战是一致的。挫折感、行为变化
和改进建议之间的相互作用说明了个人在应对重大政策转变
时所面临的错综复杂的动态变化。
5.2 创造性解决问题会议的解读
在创意解决问题环节，所产生的想法（图 9-12）与焦点小组
参与者所表达的挑战和挫折有着内在的联系。例如，解决遗
忘可重复使用杯子问题的想法与 "挑战与挫折 "思维导图中列
出的挑战直接相关。提高意识和鼓励采用可重复使用杯子的
主题反映了参与者对更多可持续发展倡议的关注和渴望。
从问题陈述中得出的主题类别形成了一个连贯的叙述。针对
遗忘的措施反映了参与者所面临的挑战，提高认识的战略回
应了参与者的挫折感和加深了解的愿望，鼓励采用的措施与
挑战和积极变革的愿望相一致，而有效的沟通渠道则解决了
焦点小组中确定的改善沟通的首要主题。
5.3 主要结论
从焦点小组和创造性问题解决会议中得出的一些重要结论包
括，乌特勒支大学为今后的可持续发展政策变革提出了若干
建议：
•
为乌特勒支大学所有学生免费提供可重复使用的杯
子
•
加强有关未来环境倡议的交流
•
与研究协会合作，获得更多支持
5.4 局限性和未来工作
本研究的局限性是反思研究结果时必须承认的一个重要方面
。焦点小组和创造性问题解决环节都提供了对学生观点的多
方面洞察，但由于其纯粹的定性性质和样本量较小，可能无
法捕捉到全部观点。创造性问题解决产生了非常有创意的解
决方案，但并非所有解决方案都现实可行。此外，即使采用
了随机抽样，学生的代表性也不能代表大学的所有学生。今
后的研究可以使用定量方法（如调查）来补充这些研究结果
，从而更广泛地了解学生的态度。未来工作的机会在于可能
实施所提出的建议并评估其影响。后续的焦点小组或访谈以
及定量方法可以全面了解所提建议的效果。

6
结 论
总之，本研究探讨了乌特勒支大学一次性杯子废物这一紧迫
的环境问题，特别关注了最近取消校园咖啡机中一次性杯子
的政策变化。研究问题旨在探讨这一政策变化如何影响学生
对乌特勒支科学园环境可持续性干预措施的看法。研究采用
了两种不同的定性研究方法，即焦点小组和创造性问题解决
会议，以收集学生对取消一次性杯子的态度、行为和挑战的
细微洞察。
焦点小组的调查结果揭示了学生们的不同反应。一些学生对
政策变化表示不满，认为难以适应，而且不便记住可重复使
用的杯子。另一些学生则接受了这一改变，认为这只是小小
的不便，却能带来巨大的环保收益。改进建议包括向所有学
生免费提供可重复使用的杯子，加强有关环保措施的沟通，
以及与学生组织合作。
创造性的问题解决会议采用 Synectics 流程方法，在四个问题
陈述中产生了大量想法。解决可重复使用杯子被遗忘问题的
策略包括在不同地点购买咖啡，以及自己动手制作杯子等非
常规方法。提高对环境影响的认识涉及利用宣传空间和展示
可视化，如展示二氧化碳减排。鼓励采用可重复使用的杯子
，包括通过校内商店提高可获得性，淘汰纸杯等替代品，以
及实施定价策略，如免费提供可重复使用的杯子或将其定价
低于 2 欧元。倡导可持续实践的有效沟通渠道包括社交媒体
平台、大学服务器和口头沟通渠道。
总之，研究强调了学生观点的多样性，并为乌特勒支大学在
完善和实施可持续政策时提供了可操作的见解。研究强调了
有针对性的沟通、替代品的可获得性以及激励可持续行为的
重要性，这些都是培养更具环保意识的校园社区的关键因素
。这些研究结果为当前关于教育机构在塑造学生可持续行为
和态度方面的作用的讨论做出了贡献。

ABSTRACT  
This study examines the impact of a sustainability policy change 
at the University of Utrecht, specifically focusing on the 
elimination of disposable cups from coffee machines on campus. 
The research investigates students' perceptions of environmental 
sustainability interventions. Employing qualitative research 
methods, including focus groups and creative problem-solving 
sessions, the study delves into the diverse responses of students to 
this policy alteration. Findings reveal a spectrum of reactions, 
with some students expressing frustrations and others endorsing 
the change for its environmental benefits. Creative problem-
solving sessions generate innovative ideas related to forgotten 
reusable cups, awareness heightening, promoting reusable cup 
adoption, and effective communication channels. Several 
recommendations for the UU include providing students with a 
free reusable cup and enhanced communication strategies. The 
study contributes valuable insights for Utrecht University and 
similar institutions aiming to cultivate environmentally conscious 
campus communities. 
KEYWORDS  
Environmental Interventions, Disposable Cups, Student 
Perceptions, Focus Groups, Creative Problem Solving, Campus 
Sustainability, Policy Change. 
1 INTRODUCTION  
1.1 Problem Domain 
Environmental concerns have witnessed a notable increase 
globally [4], prompting institutions worldwide, Utrecht University 
included, 
to 
reevaluate their 
sustainability 
policies. 
An 
unpublished report from the Facilities Service Center in 2018 [3] 
revealed that annually, over 1.5 million paper cups, each 
comprising a layer of polyethylene plastic, are discarded within 
the university premises [3]. This revelation resulted in the 
adoption of a new sustainable policy for Utrecht University, 
entailing that all single-use cups from coffee machines were 
removed since November 2023. Due to this shift, students from 
Utrecht University are required to bring their own cups. As a 
result of this policy alteration, diverse reactions from peers have 
surfaced, with some frequently neglecting to bring their cups, 
consequently 
experiencing 
frustration, 
while 
others 
enthusiastically endorse the change, underscoring an augmented 
environmental awareness. 
1.2 Research Question 
The aforementioned environmental concern necessitates an 
investigation into effective strategies to influence students' 
behaviour in the context of sustainable practices, particularly the 
recent elimination of disposable cups from campus coffee 
machines at Utrecht Science Park. The central research question 
guiding this inquiry is as follows: 
RQ: How does the recent elimination of disposable cups at 
campus coffee machines influence students' perceptions of 
environmental sustainability interventions at Utrecht Science 
Park? 
The research scope is delimited to the specific realm of 
environmental sustainability interventions related to the removal 
of disposable cups. Two distinct approaches are employed to 
address this research question. The first approach involves 
mapping the reasons behind students' responses to the elimination 
of disposable cups, with a focus on identifying obstacles and 
emphasizing 
aspects 
that 
may 
stimulate 
environmentally 
sustainable behaviours. This indirect approach is facilitated 
through the application of semi-structured focus groups as the 
qualitative research method, providing nuanced insights into the 
motivations and challenges faced by students. 
Concurrently, the second approach takes a direct exploration into 
the perspectives of students regarding factors that could influence 
their adoption of more sustainable practices. In this case, the 
qualitative research method of creative problem solving (CPS) is 
integrated. By combining these methodologies, a comprehensive 
understanding of students' attitudes, behaviours, and challenges in 
response to the elimination of disposable cups is sought. 
The culmination of this research aims to provide foundational 
insights that can inform the development of techniques to 
encourage sustainable practices within the university context. 
Future research endeavours can build upon the knowledge gained 
from these approaches, potentially leading to a more informed and 
responsive university governance. This heightened understanding 
of students' perspectives could facilitate the formulation and

   2   BACKGROUND  
Given the aforementioned research question and approaches, 
several key aspects are relevant for the theoretical background of 
this study. The focus of this investigation is on understanding how 
the recent elimination of disposable cups at campus coffee 
machines influences students' perceptions of environmental 
sustainability interventions at Utrecht Science Park. This shift in 
policy at Utrecht University, aimed at reducing environmental 
impact, provides an opportunity to explore the dynamics of 
behavioural change among students. Previous studies by Emanuel 
& Adams [2] and Levy & Marans [6] will serve valuable insights 
in the exploration of the role of educational institutions in shaping 
sustainable practices and attitudes. 
 
In the study by Emanuel & Adams [2], it was found that student’s 
sustainability commitment levels were influenced by their local 
communities. The need for proactive involvement from college 
administrators was underscored, urging them to lead sustainable 
efforts on campus to reduce the commitment gap, entailing the 
gap between student’s desire to practice sustainable manners but 
not fully committing to them. In essence, this paper underscores 
the crucial role of educational institutions in driving policy 
changes and promoting a culture of sustainability among their 
students.  
Globally, many universities have adopted similar policy changes 
to enhance sustainability and awareness among their students. The 
University of Michigan in the United States, for example, has 
taken a different approach to environmental sustainability within 
campus communities by addressing cultural and behavioural 
dimensions The culture team of this university proposed 
educational and training programs to enhance issue-based 
knowledge while suggesting specific engagement strategies, 
coupled with social incentives, to encourage environmentally 
sustainable behaviour [6]. The authors of this paper underscore 
the importance of institutions recognizing the cultural aspects of 
environmental sustainability. They argue that widespread 
awareness and engagement in sustainability can lead to long-term 
progress in waste reduction [6].  
This study makes a significant contribution to the ongoing 
discourse regarding the pivotal role of educational institutions in 
shaping sustainable practices and attitudes among students. The 
recent policy alteration at Utrecht University serves as a pertinent 
case study, providing an opportunity to delve into the pragmatic 
implications of such interventions. Through the implementation of 
semi-structured focus groups and creative problem-solving 
sessions, this research aims to capture the diverse perspectives of 
students, elucidating the factors that influence their responses to 
the removal of disposable cups. These qualitative methods 
facilitate an exploration of the nuanced dimensions inherent in 
students' perceptions and behaviors, thereby offering valuable 
insights for both Utrecht University and analogous institutions 
grappling with sustainability challenges. The relevance of this 
study therefore lies in its potential to inform forthcoming 
sustainability initiatives within educational institutions and 
contribute to a broader understanding of effective strategies for 
fostering sustainable behavior among students. 
 3   STUDY DESIGN  
3.1 Methods 
   3.1.1 Focus Groups In this study, two semi-structured  focus 
groups were employed to explore and understand students' 
perceptions of the recent policy change at the university – the 
removal of disposable cups. This method, inspired by Breen’s [1]  
stages and Krueger’s [5] framework, aimed to gather in-depth 
insights into student’s experiences with the transition to reusable 
cups. The focus groups were designed to explore various 
components,  including initial thoughts, discuss behavioural 
changes, identify strengths and challenges of the elimination, and 
explore broader impacts on sustainability.  
The semi-structured format provided a balance between 
predetermined questions and opportunities for spontaneous 
discussions. This method facilitated the gathering of diverse 
perspectives on the elimination of disposable cups and its impact 
on campus sustainability. 
 
   3.1.2 Creative Problem Solving  In this study, two Creative 
Problem Solving (CPS) session were employed. CPS sessions 
represent a structured group brainstorming approach, focused on 
generating ideas and solutions for identified alterations within the 
context of this study. Diverging from alternative methodologies, 
CPS sessions prioritise ideation over the collection of diverse data 
pertaining to students' opinions, experiences and behaviours. 
Consequently, CPS sessions prove to be particularly effective in 
producing a higher quantity of ideas within a relatively condensed 
timeframe. Notably, this method places emphasis on cultivating 
inventive and unconventional ideas, a quality that is particularly 
pertinent for this study, which seeks to obtain a comprehensive 
understanding of the perspectives of university students in 
response to the elimination of disposable cups. 
The adaptability of CPS sessions is evidenced by the various 
approaches that can be employed. In alignment with the objectives 
of this study, the Synectics process approach was chosen, with 
specifically the career excursion and the street excursion. The 
Synectic process approach is underpinned by the central idea of 
engaging both conscious and subconscious minds to unveil 
connections that might elude discovery through a purely rational 
framework [7]. By applying the Synectics process approach within 
the CPS sessions, the aim is to tap into the creative faculties of 
students and explore innovative solutions that might be otherwise 
overlooked.

3.2 Participants 
For both the focus groups and creative problem solving sessions, 
this study employed random sampling as the method of 
participant selection. The targeted population encompassed all 
Dutch citizens, chosen based on two specific criteria:  
1) 
Their coffee consumption behaviour on campus. 
2) 
Their awareness of the discontinuation of disposable 
cups at the coffee vending machines on campus. 
 
The sampling frame was comprised of individuals within the 
researchers' environment, specifically Utrecht University students, 
aligning seamlessly with the intended target demographic. A total 
of two focus groups and two Creative Problem Solving (CPS) 
sessions were conducted, each involving six participants. 
Due to the constraints imposed by the available time frame and 
the attained level of saturation in responses, the researchers opted 
not to conduct any additional focus groups or CPS sessions, 
concluding the total number of "investigations" at four (two focus 
groups and two CPS sessions).

campus classrooms, fostering a clear discussion. After obtaining

2) 
Introductory questions 
3) 
Transition questions 
4)

5)

Afterwards, the recordings were transcribed using Microsoft 
Word. For coding and analysing the data, NVivo (version 12), 
a software program for qualitative data analysis was used. 
Lastly, mind maps were created using a mind mapping 
software.  
 
   3.3.2 Creative Problem Solving The Creative Problem 
Solving (CPS) session took place in an unoccupied classroom. 
Verbal consent was obtained from participants for the 
utilisation of their information within the scope of this study. 
The decision against written consent was made in 
consideration of the established relationship with the selected 
participants. Given the broad nature of the research question, it 
was refined into four distinct problem statements, namely: 
1) 
What measures should be implemented when an 
individual forgets their reusable cup? 
2) 
How can awareness regarding the environmental impact 
of disposable cups be heightened to induce behavioural 
change? 
3) 
In what manner can campus coffee establishments 
promote and incentivise the adoption of reusable cups? 
4) 
Which communication channels are most effective in 
advocating sustainable practices? 
 
These problem statements were succinctly noted on the centre of 
whiteboards, each allocated for a specific statement to allow 
sufficient space for ideation. Participants were equipped with their 
individual whiteboard markers for recording ideas. Additionally, a 
laptop was present for the researchers to take notes and review 
information related to excursions. 
The career excursion occurred within the confines of the 
classroom, while for the street excursion, participants circuited the 
university building. Subsequent analysis employed NVivo 
(version 12), a qualitative data analysis software, to discern

3.4 Procedure 
   3.4.1 Focus Groups The focus group sessions followed the 
following procedure: first, the participants were welcomed, and 
gratitude was expressed for their attendance. This was followed 
by the introduction of the researchers, one acting as moderator 
and the other as note-taker to create a friendly and informal 
atmosphere. An overview of the research purpose was presented, 
along with a request for consent to record the audio. The 
discussion comprised opening questions to explore initial 
thoughts, introductory questions on behavioural changes, 
transition questions about strengths and challenges, key questions 
addressing broader impacts, and ending questions for final 
thoughts. The closing part of the focus groups involved expressing 
gratitude, offering to share final thoughts, and seeking feedback 
for future improvements. The duration of both focus groups was 
approximately 45 minutes. The complete protocol, including 
questions and detailed time schedule, can be found in Appendix 
A. 
   3.4.2 Creative Problem Solving As previously mentioned, the 
CPS method was executed twice, once with each group, and each 
execution comprised two Synectics Process sessions. Initially, 
participants were thanked for their time and participation. 
Subsequently, they were introduced to the main topics and aims of 
the research and were briefed on the planned procedure of the 
sessions. The previously introduced problem statements were 
simultaneously addressed, and two Synectics Process excursions 
were conducted. The following steps were undertaken in each 
session: 
1) 
The session commenced with an introduction of the 
four respective problem statements displayed at the 
centre of the whiteboard. 
2) 
Participants were then prompted to generate ideas 
for the respective problem statements. 
3) 
After the initial ideas were documented on the 
whiteboard, the first excursion was introduced – the 
career excursion. 
4) 
Participants 
assumed 
the 
roles 
of 
various 
professions, including firefighter, teacher, lawyer, 
constructor, doctor, and banker. The purpose of this 
excursion 
was 
to 
rejuvenate 
and 
stimulate 
participants' creativity by diverting their minds from 
the focused topics and problem statements. 
5) 
Subsequently, participants were asked, "How does it 
feel to be a…?" Following this excursion, the session 
resumed, and participants could once again note 
down ideas. 
6) 
Once participants had completed their ideation, the 
street excursion ensued, during which participants 
took a stroll around the university building. This was 
followed by the final round of idea generation.

on the environmental impact of disposable cups, and showcasing 
the CO2 emission reduction following the removal of disposable 
cups.

reusable cups on campus, eliminating alternatives like paper cups, 
holding a win action, giving reusable cups as Christmas gifts, and 
reducing the price of reusable cups. Pricing-related ideas 
encompassed options such as charging for the use of reusable 
cups, offering a free cookie or snack with the purchase of a 
reusable cup, making reusable cups free, and pricing them below 
two euros.

Facebook. Ideas for UU servers included utilizing UU mail, DUB 
(the digital university magazine), and incorporating mentions in 
lectures. Verbal promotion ideas comprised using the radio, word-
of-mouth advertising, and performances.

5.1 Interpretation of Focus Group Findings 
The findings from the focus groups provided a multifaceted 
understanding of student’s reactions to the recent policy change at 
Utrecht University. Figure 3 visually represents the distribution of 
participant responses across key thematic categories. Notably, the 
majority expressed frustrations with the policy change, 
emphasizing challenges and concerns. However, a significant 
portion also supported the policy, particularly in the context of 
environmental considerations. Suggestions for improvement 
related to communication strategies were also prominent, along 
with discussions about behavioural changes attributed to the 
policy change.  
The mind maps (Figures 4-7) offer a deeper exploration of each 
category, outlining subthemes and recurring responses. In the 
"Challenges and Frustrations" category, participants expressed 
difficulties in adapting to the new policy, including forgetfulness 
and practical inconveniences. This directly influenced the 
"Behavioural Changes" category, where participants discussed 
shifts in habits, such as increased use of reusable cups but 
occasional forgetfulness. Simultaneously, the positive attitudes 
captured in the "Pro-policy for Environmental Sustainability" 
category showcased a complex relationship between challenges 
and the overarching commitment to sustainability. 
Moreover, participants' frustrations and challenges provided the 
backdrop for their "Suggestions for Improvement." The need for 
effective communication strategies emerged as a central theme, 
aligning with the challenges participants faced in adapting to the 
policy change. The interplay between frustration, behavioural 
changes, and suggestions for improvement illustrates the intricate 
dynamics of individuals grappling with and responding to a 
significant policy shift. 
 
 
5.2 Interpretation of Creative Problem Solving Sessions 
Transitioning to the creative problem solving session, the 
generated ideas (Figure 9-12) are inherently linked to the 
challenges and frustrations expressed by participants in the focus

directly correlate with the challenges outlined in the "Challenges

incentivising reusable cup adoption reflect participants' concerns 
and desires for more accessible sustainability initiatives. 
The thematic categories derived from the problem statements 
form a cohesive narrative. Measures for forgetfulness reflect the 
challenges participants faced, awareness strategies respond to the 
frustrations and desires for greater understanding, incentivising 
adoption aligns with both challenges and aspirations for positive 
change, and effective communication channels address the 
overarching theme of improved communication identified in the

6 CONCLUSION  
In conclusion, this study addressed the pressing environmental 
concern of disposable cup waste at Utrecht University, 
specifically focusing on the recent policy change eliminating 
disposable cups from campus coffee machines. The research 
question aimed to explore how this policy alteration influenced 
students' perceptions of environmental sustainability interventions 
at Utrecht Science Park. Two distinct qualitative research 
methods, focus groups, and creative problem-solving sessions, 
were employed to gather nuanced insights into students' attitudes, 
behaviours, and challenges in response to the elimination of 
disposable cups. 
The findings from the focus groups revealed a spectrum of 
responses among students. Some expressed frustrations with the 
policy 
change, 
citing 
difficulties 
in 
adapting 
and 
the 
inconvenience of remembering reusable cups. Others embraced 
the change, recognizing it as a small inconvenience for a 
significant environmental gain. Suggestions for improvement 
included providing free reusable cups to all students, enhancing 
communication about environmental initiatives, and collaborating 
with student organizations. 
The creative problem-solving sessions, utilising the Synectics 
process approach, generated a wealth of ideas across four problem 
statements. Strategies to address forgotten reusable cups ranged 
from acquiring coffee at different locations to unconventional 
methods like do-it-yourself cups. Heightening awareness of the 
environmental impact involved utilizing publicity spaces and 
presenting visualizations, such as showcasing CO2 emission 
reduction. Encouraging the adoption of reusable cups included 
enhancing accessibility through on-campus shops, eliminating 
alternatives like paper cups, and implementing pricing strategies, 
such as making reusable cups free or pricing them below two 
euros. 
Effective 
communication 
channels 
for 
advocating 
sustainable practices spanned social media platforms, university 
servers, and verbal communication channels. 
In summary, the study highlighted the diversity of student 
perspectives and provided actionable insights for Utrecht 
University to consider in refining and implementing sustainable 
policies. The importance of targeted communication, accessibility 
of alternatives, and incentivizing sustainable behavior were 
underscored as key factors in fostering a more environmentally 
conscious campus community. The findings contribute to the 
ongoing discourse on the role of educational institutions in 
shaping sustainable practices and attitudes among students.

3.5.1
焦点小组。两个焦点小组的录音均已自动转录为书面
文本。必要时对这些文字记录进行人工调整。使用 Microsoft 
Word，按照施特劳斯基础理论的开放式和轴向编码方法对文
字记录进行编码，从而从收集的数据中构建新的理论[23]。
每种编码都有引文支持，以证明每种编码。两份记录誊本最
初都只由一名研究人员进行编码，采用的是拆分编码策略
[20]。在对两份记录誊本进行编码后，研究人员交换记录誊
本以进行验证，并对所使用的代码进行统一。对任何不一致
之处进行讨论以达成共识，然后将编码合并到一个编码本中
。之所以选择这种拆分编码方法，是因为这种方法对于小型
团队来说相对高效，而且第二个验证步骤可确保编码的准确性和可
靠性[20]。在开放式编码阶段，记录誊本的相关片段被归类为代码
，用简短的句子表达其含义。这些代码是通过新兴编码法得出的。
新兴编码是一个归纳过程，即编码从收集到的数据中发现的概念中
产生，而不是由既定理论预先确定。然后，将开放式编码归类为焦
点小组讨论的主题。例如，"支持购买二手车的原因 "或 "对购买二
手车的看法"。

3.5.2
CPS.创造性问题解决环节的数据记录在便签上
。在小组评估过程中，讨论要点被写在相应的便笺上
，并在 1-5 之间进行排序。下一步是对便笺进行筛选
和数字化处理，具体做法是将解决方案及其讨论要点
手动输入 Excel 表格。重复或明显错误的解决方案会被
过滤掉。所有数据传输完毕后，使用 SWOT 矩阵对每
个解决方案进行分析。SWOT 矩阵或分析被称为战略
规划工具，通常用于商业领域[8]。SWOT 分析确定并
评估优势、劣势、机会和威胁[8]。在本例中，优势和
劣势代表了有助于或挑战解决方案应用的明确内部因
素。机会和威胁则代表可能导致解决方案产生积极或
消极结果的外部因素或情况[8]。每个解决方案都根据
其对问题的总体影响进行了排名。排序范围为 1-5，1 
表示影响小，5 表示影响大。最初的排序由两位研究人
员进行审核，必要时进行调整。最后，所有想法都根
据 "类型 "和 "类别 "进行了分类。"类型 "是指解决方案
的类型（如网络应用功能），"类别 "是指根据解决方
案的 （如 "用户体验与便利性"）。

店代为销售。此解决方案归类为 "用户体验与便利性 "下
的 "战略"。
理论对应 据估计，这一解决方案将提升购物体验 (4)，改善
便利性和物流限制 (6)，解决焦点小组提出的几大瓶颈问
题。首先，顾客可以试穿服装，服装质量将直接可见。
第三方的参与解决了销售和购买过程中的许多物流难题
（如图像和沟通不畅）。然而，由于失去了一定的便利
性，一些喜欢网上购物的客户群将会流失。此外，这需
要大规模的运作，因此不易实现。由于新的成本，价格
可能会上涨。
影响估计为 4，因为该解决方案被认为具有直接可见的影响。实
体店将改善外联工作，接触到新的客户群体。总体而言，该
解决方案将提高外联、透明度和认知度。
4.2.2
显示生态影响的布方
描述 商品列表旁的信息按钮，显示购买该商品对生态环境的影响。
该信息可以根据品牌考虑原产地，也可以显示更多有关有意识
消费的一般信息。该解决方案被归类为 "透明度和意识 "下的 "
功能"。

--- Page 14 ---
14
理论对应 该解决方案与人们关心环境的意愿和道德原
因有关（1, 3）。许多人在焦点小组中表达了对服
装来源的担忧，认为即使是二手服装也不一定符
合道德或环保要求（例如，高消耗）。这种易于
实施的解决方案符合人们对透明度的高度需求。
然而，获取此类数据可能具有挑战性，解决方案
的影响可能低于预期，用户可能会怀疑是在 "洗
绿"。
影响 本解决方案的影响估计为
3.它利用了重要的价值观和 "激励 "技术。总之，
显示生态影响的按钮将提高透明度和意识。
4.2.3
负责品牌和商店，销售自有品牌的二手和/或停产
商品。
描述 该功能允许品牌在 C2C 上建立用户账户。
这些账户专门用于出售旧衣物，这些衣物是通过捐赠收
集的，以换取金钱/𝑏𝑎𝑐𝑘𝑠𝑙𝑎𝑠馁credits。
其他物品，如停产或退货物品，也可以
这样销售。该解决方案既面向高端品牌，也面向低
端品牌。该解决方案被归类为 "用户体验与便利性 
"下的 "战略"。
理论对应 该解决方案符合人们对环保的关注 (1)，提升
了（在线）购物体验 (4)，并解决了用户的不便和
物流问题。首先，品牌可以提供质量更高和描述性
更强的商品列表，商品的尺寸和合身度也更可靠（
因为用户有使用已知品牌的经验）。品牌通常风格
更一致，使用户更容易找到自己想要的东西。这种
解决方案可利用消费者的忠诚度，并可增加推广量
。但是，这种解决方案依赖于品牌的合作，而这种
合作可能无法保证。高端品牌可能不愿意与这种循
环经济合作，而低端品牌可能无法提供可重复使用
的优质服装。对某些品牌来说，可能没有足够的利
润。
影响估计为 5，假设至少有一定数量的企业愿意合作。
成功实施这一具有说服力的战略可降低购买二手商品
的门槛，吸引新的客户群体，提高环保意识。

4.2.4
根据款式而不是项目组进行分类。
描述 不再根据商品类别（如裤子）对列表进行分类，而是根
据风格（如 Y2K、grunge）对商品进行分类。这种有说服
力的技术被归类为 "算法 "下的 "功能"。
理论对应 这种有说服力的技术增强了购物体验（4），并为展
示和推荐服装商品提供了另一种方法（5）。研究结果表
明，许多人不喜欢 C2C 上的算法，这使得在没有特定想
法的情况下寻找新商品变得非常困难。通过根据风格定位
对服装进行排序，用户可以在不改变个性化推荐的情况
下探索各种风格。该功能是对商品组分类的补充，而不
是取代。它将增强用户的网上购物体验，提供一种新的
应用程序使用方式，如探索个人风格偏好。然而，风格
是很难归类的，尤其是考虑到图片
的列表往往缺乏质量。如果该功能不能正常工作，就有可能
使界面不必要地复杂化。
影响估计为 4，因为 C2C 上的推荐系统经常被认为是网上
购买二手服装的一个挑战因素。众所周知，推荐系统对
用户参与度有重大影响。
4.2.5
人工智能驱动的造型师机器人。
描述 将人工智能驱动的造型师（聊天）机器人纳入购物体验
。该机器人可进行高级搜索，例如用自然语言描述商品。
它在推进当前推荐系统的同时，还能作为个人助理提供帮
助，例如，通过查找个人风格、创建列表或查找具有相似
个人风格的用户。这被归类为 "算法 "下的 "功能"。
理论对应 与基于风格的分类类似，该功能可增强购物体验（4
），并高度改善商品列表的展示和推荐（5）。同样，人
工智能聊天机器人将为 C2C 平台提供另一项功能，即寻
找个人风格。传统上，机器人可以为用户推荐赠品。但
是，人工智能聊天机器人的安装和维护需要大量计算
。人工智能可能缺乏人情味，因此风格建议有可能导
致失望而非满意。
影响估计为 3.因为它可能不会直接影响环境因素，因此可能不会
产生直接影响。不过，将人工智能成功整合到平台中，有可
能大大提高用户友好度。

案可以改善用户体验，但可能无法直接改善环境。
4.2./ 为卖家提供自取包裹服务。
说明 这种有说服力的策略不是由卖家自己包装、印制标
签并送到包装点，而是提供上门取货服务，减轻卖家
出售二手服装物品的负担。这项服务由 C2C 平台提
供，如果是小距离送货，送货员可能会驾驶电动自行
车。该解决方案被归入 "用户体验与便利性 "下的 "战
略 "类别。
理论对应 该解决方案缓解了销售二手服装时的不便和
物流问题（6），并解决了环境问题（1）。与会
者表示在网上销售服装存在心理障碍。实施这一策
略将延长送货时间，使此类平台的销售更具吸引力
，从而促进 C2C 平台的活跃度。但是，如果送货距
离更远，则会增加二氧化碳排放量。相关的运营工
作和成本也会增加，用户可能会面临采用问题。
总体而言，这一战略为卖方和买方提供了更多的便利，
有助于环境 精神方面的考虑。
5
讨论
本文研究了消费者在消费者对消费者平台上购买二手服装的
动机。随后，我们回顾了七种说服策略，以应对网上购买二
手服装的相关挑战。首先，我们发现参与者非常关注快速时
尚对环境的影响。其次，我们发现参与者认为二手服装通常
质量较好，这是购买服装时的一个重要考虑因素。第三，除
了环境因素外，最重要的因素是道德（对人权的关注）、物
质

--- Page 17 ---
17
(价格和质量）和社会（社会影响）性质。关于网上二手服装
，我们发现参与者在决定购买网上二手服装还是店内二手服
装时，非常重视购物体验。参与者不喜欢平台展示和推荐服
装商品的方式。最后，参与者认为 C2C 平台的不便和物流困
难阻碍了他们的发展。根据这些发现，我们提出了七种说服
技巧：C2C 平台的弹出式商店、生态影响按钮、C2C 平台上
的品牌账户、基于风格的分类、人工智能驱动的造型师机器
人、列表下方的讨论区以及为卖家提供的提货包裹服务。
根据 Jaman 等人（2019 年）的研究结果，我们预计会发现不
同的消费者群体，每个群体都持有不同的价值观。相反，人
们倾向于持有多种属性。具体来说，当一个人决定是否购买
二手物品时，道德、物质和社会动机是重要的考虑因素。这
些研究结果与之前的研究结果基本一致，即经济、环境、道
德和享乐是二手购物的主要驱动因素[5, 6, 9, 10]。主要区别
在于如何对动机进行分类和命名。在本研究中，环境和道德
因素被视为同义词，而经济原因则被更广泛地解释为 "物欲"
。尽管文献中对动机的分类方式存在差异，但它们都表明，
决策者很可能同时持有自我利益和道德考虑的混合价值观。
我们的研究结果也强调了这一观点。
此外，这项研究还表明，人们在决定在网上购买二手商品
还是在实体店购买时，非常看重购物体验。如果购物体验不
佳，人们就会放弃购买二手商品。从这个意义上说，购物体
验也可以被视为一种动机，与早期研究中定义的享乐主义原
因密切相关[5, 10]。同样，现有研究证实，消费者的习惯并
不容易概括。例如，与网购相比，在店内购物的消费者在价
值观上存在细微差别。因此，CPS 提出的大多数特征都来
自与购物体验相关的焦点小组调查结果（表 2 中的 4、5 
和 6）。将特征与调查结果 1、2 和 3 联系起来更具挑战性
，因为这些特征与个人价值观、观点或服装本身的性质有
关。例如，认为二手服装质量高，但有时可能外观不佳，
这是二手商品的固有特征，很难用 C2C 特征对其施加影响。
相反，与购物体验有关的方面，如不便或物流限制，则与 
C2C 平台直接相关，因此更容易通过引入/改变功能和/或策
略来解决。
显然，我们的参与者认识到了可持续购物行为对环境的紧迫性。然
而，几乎所有的参与者都表示，他们仍然每月至少购买一次快时尚
产品。这种价值观和行为的不一致性与当前有关环保一致性行为的
实验研究形成了鲜明对比。

18
1] 。同时，其他研究也没有发现这种相关性[21]。
Thøgersen （2004 年）的一项研究表明，一般来说，人
们会表现出与环境相一致的行为，但当面临约束时，
就会出现不一致的情况（例如，如果没有其他选择，
人们不可能总是选择乘火车旅行）[24]。对许多人来说
，只要存在这样的界限，完全戒掉快时尚消费可能并
不是一个可行的选择。我们的研究显示，购买二手服
装确实存在某些障碍，这进一步强调了干预的必要性
，如 本文提出的策略。
5.1
局限性和未来工作
尽管这项研究证实了一些发现，并为研究领域提供
了更多依据，但仍有几个局限性值得一提。首先，
创造性问题解决方法的筛选程序容易受到主观因素的
影响。尽管我们的目的是以我们能想到的最系统的方
式进行选择，但并不能保证这些选择结果是最优的
。首先，每个解决方案的排序属性虽然是基于 SWOT 
分析，但本质上是一种主观衡量标准（此外，SWOT 
分析的客观性也会受到批评）。与研究结果的关系也
是如此；没有对任何研究结果赋予权重。根据与理论
关系的数量来进行选择，忽视了某些发现可能比其他
发现更深刻。对于未来的研究而言，最重要的是不要
止步于记录解决方案，而是要继续测试和实施每个
解决方案，以确保干预措施的有效性。否则，我们强
烈建议重新寻求更严格的创造性问题解决分析标准。
此外，本文的定性研究性质也带来了额外的挑战。如
前所述，定性研究本身具有主观性。由于小组动态的
复杂性，焦点小组会带来参与者相互影响的风险。这
尤其给本研究减少偏差带来了困难。起初，我们认为
可持续发展和二手衣物的话题并不敏感。然而，在访
谈过程中，参与者之间出现了关于道德问题的争论，
在这些争论中，我们发现了对彼此答案的判断。这尤
其表明，社会期望值的作用可能比预期的要大。虽然
定性研究具有探索和深入了解详细经验的优势，但下一步将
采取更有条理的方法。在未来的工作中，网上 C2C 行为这一
主题可以采用正式的测量方法进行评估，例如结合观察数据
（如购物行为）评估环境意识的量表。这将有助于发现相关
模式，例如环保意识是否反映在行为中。
如前所述，在本研究中，我们没有发现存在不同客户群的确
凿证据，因为这不是研究的主要重点。通过今后在实验环境
中开展的研究工作，对这方面进行更详细的探索可能是一个
有趣的途径。

19
最后，由于本研究的样本只有 9 人，因此本研究的结果很
难推广到更大的人群中。此外，样本大多由已经参与环保
等话题的进步学生组成。虽然我们从参与者身上获得了有
用的见解，但他们不太可能代表更广泛的人群。因此，在今

程度可能会特别有意义。
6
结 论
总之，本研究探讨了在消费者对消费者平台上促进二手服装
参与的说服技巧。通过焦点小组和创造性问题解决会议，我
们更清楚地了解了人们的看法，为旨在鼓励购买二手服装的
新解决方案提供了基础。我们提出了七种最有可能对二手服
装平台的参与产生积极影响的说服技巧。我们的研究为现有
文献做出了贡献，为二手服装观念的改变提供了补充支持。
随着人们环保意识的提高和对新服装来源的关注，二手服装
越来越受欢迎。消费者之间的在线平台使二手物品的买卖更
加方便，因此也受到了人们的欢迎。不过，我们的研究也发
现了在线二手市场需要改进的地方，尤其是在便利性、物流
和整体购物体验方面。如果不加以改进，二手市场将继续保
持高门槛，使其难以与快速时尚有效竞争。尽管如此，由于
当前服装业对环境的影响，可持续时尚仍然是一个日益相关
的话题[16]，而选择二手服装而不是新服装已被证明是一种
可持续的选择[11]。对许多人来说，完全戒掉快速时尚消费
可能并不是一个可行的选择，正如本研究所示，尽管人们充
分认识到二手商品的重要性，但购买二手商品的障碍仍然很
大。这进一步强调了采取干预措施的必要性，比如本文提出
的说服技巧。

Digitally Sustainable: Understanding Consumer Behavior on
Second-hand Clothing Platforms
**

ABSTRACT
Despite a growing awareness of the detrimental effects disposable
fashion has on the environment, consumers continue to seek out
fast fashion [13]. In effort of building a circular economy, consumer-
to-consumer (C2C) second-hand clothing platforms have grown
popular [17, 28]. Academic attention has not yet caught up with
these recent developments, therefore little documentation exists
of consumer attitudes, behaviors and values towards second-hand
clothing (online) [11]. Therefore the scope of this qualitative study
is to capture consumer motivations when buying second-hand
clothing on such platforms. By combining focus groups with cre-
ative problem solving techniques, this paper contributes to existing
literature by providing additional support for users’ motivations
when buying second-hand clothing (online). Additionally, seven
persuasive strategies were reviewed which tackle the bottlenecks
which users experience. Though participants recognized the envi-
ronmental urgency of sustainable shopping behavior, this is not
always reflected in consistent behavior, underscoring the relevance
of persuasive interventions. For future research, obtaining quantita-
tive data is essential to strengthen our understanding of conscious
shopping behavior.

INTRODUCTION
From traditionally four fashion seasons per year, fast fashion com-
panies are now cycling through approximately 20-30 seasons every
year [2]. Taking it even further, fashion retailers such as Shein can
produce items in less than three days after a trend has been iden-
tified, and launch at least 8000 new items on their site every day
[14]. Disposable fashion is rapidly growing despite the well-known
environmental impacts; from chemical pollution, water use, C02
emissions from global supply chains to textile waste [16].
Fundamental changes are needed to transition back to slow fashion;
despite consumers’ interest in sustainability, they continue to seek
out fast fashion [13]. Sustainable fashion at low costs is hardly
attainable in competition with fast fashion retailers [18]. Drivers
like low costs and sustainability potentially spike an interest in
the circular economy of alternative consumption for secondhand
clothing [18].

Evidently, consumers hold leverage by consuming less new cloth-
ing, favouring sustainable brands over fast fashion or opting for
second-hand clothing [13, 27]. However, there has not been much
academic attention to consumer attitudes, behaviors and values
towards second hand clothing (online) [11].
2
BACKGROUND
Fast fashion systems are defined by their brief production cycles,
rapid distribution and the incorporation of highly fashionable
designs, relying on cheap manufacturing and low garment quality
[4]. Swift distribution is achieved using localized production and
advanced information systems, enabling a retailer to monitor and
replenish their inventory continuously [4]. Fast fashion retailers
continuously monitor consumer and industry trends to speed up
production and distribution [4].
Consumers can decrease demand for fast fashion by buying second-
hand clothing instead [13, 27], considering a significant proportion
of garments are discarded even when they are still wearable [7].
When comparing to recycling and re-purposing clothing, the reuse
of clothing is the most environmentally friendly option [11, 27].
This is due to the fact that prolonging the active use of clothing by
only nine months already holds potential to reduce environmental
impacts related to water waste and carbon footprints by roughly
20% to 30%. [27]. Expected, people will substitute second-hand
clothing for new-clothing, rather than accumulate consumption of
both [7]. Research showed that purchasing second-hand clothing
is estimated to reduce new clothing purchases greatly; for every
100 second-hand second-hand garments, consumption of 60 to
85 new clothing pieces are spared [7]. Though consuming sec-
ond hand clothing has its own environmental costs as well, the
environmental effects of collecting, processing, and transporting
second-hand clothing are found to be minimal when compared to
the substantial savings attained by replacing newly manufactured
clothing [7].
Traditionally, buying second-hand clothing was primarily associ-
ated with lower income classes, where buying second-hand was
out of economic necessity [26]. However, in recent years a shift
in perception has taken place where people choose to buy second-
hand clothing or even prefer it over new clothing [26, 28]. From
flea markets and thrift shops, to high-end vintage retailers and
highly developed online platforms [28]. Such consumer to con-
sumer (C2C) platforms are gaining prominence in the market for
pre-owned clothing; a few examples include Vinted or Etsy [17, 28].
Items are listed and are purchased directly from and sent by the
seller, using online banking [17, 28]. C2C platforms enhance the
1

--- Page 2 ---
accessibility of second-hand clothing, potentially lowering the bar-
riers for consumers to choose pre-owned items over new ones.
However, it remains uncertain whether this is indeed the case,
as there is limited research on consumer attitudes towards C2C
platforms and second-hand clothing in general.
2.1
Related work
Previous research on the attitudes towards second-hand clothing
shopping revealed that motivations for second-hand clothing are
largely economically driven [6, 9]. Besides economic reasons, en-
vironmental, ethical and hedonic reasons (second-hand shopping
for entertainment purposes) are drivers of second-hand shopping
[5, 10].
Jägel et al. (2012) formally proposed that ethical clothing con-
sumption is propelled by five dominant consumer motivations:
environmental consciousness, altruistic and ethical considerations,
well-being, economic value, and personal image. [12]. Interest-
ingly, a study by Bly et al. (2015) found rather different factors
contributing to the consumption of second-hand clothing. Blye et
al. found novelty, creativity, and appearance to be key components
consumers looked for in sustainable fashion, suggesting that peo-
ple value self-interest above ethical considerations when buying
second-hand clothing [3]. This broad range of values proposed by
existing literature may suggest two things; consumers likely hold
mixed values of both self-interest and ethical considerations when
buying second-hand clothing
In line with this speculation, a recent study by Zaman et al. (2019)
found second-hand clothing shoppers should not be labeled as
one distinct consumer group, but rather three groups with each
different orientations. For example, they found consignment store
shoppers were more motivated by nostalgia and fashion conscious-
ness than thrift store shoppers were, who were stronger motivated
by dematerialism. In turn, online shoppers were more driven by
nostalgia and fashion consciousness than thrift store shoppers [29].
These findings suggest that generalizations about consumer habits
may not apply to all, emphasizing the need for additional research
on the habits and attitudes of individuals who primarily purchase
second-hand shopping on CMC platforms. In our effort to develop
persuasive strategies for various consumer groups, we include
all audiences in our research scope. Furthermore, our goal is to
contribute to existing literature by offering insights into the habits
and attitudes of consumers towards second-hand CMC platforms.
Perhaps, a distinction in customer types requires adaptation of the
persuasive strategies.
2.2
Research Question
The scope of the current study is to capture consumer motivations
when buying second-hand clothing on consumer-to-consumer
platforms. With an improved understanding of the challenges and
advantages users encounter, we can explore persuasive solutions
which fit these needs and make a conscious effort to promoting
sustainable online shopping behavior. Therefore, the present study
aims to answer the research question:
How can persuasive techniques be used to promote purchasing and en-
gaging with second-hand clothing on consumer-to-consumer (CMC)
platforms?
This paper will establish an answer to this research question by first
understanding consumers’ motives for buying second-hand. Here
we take a broad approach and capture both motives for buying
in-store second-hand and online second-hand. We deem motives
in both instances may be valuable to a solution. Next, we explore
solutions and narrow our scope down to online second-hand shop-
ping.

METHODS & STUDY DESIGN
A total of eleven qualitative research methods have been consid-
ered to be used in this study to gain a better understanding of
our research question: How can persuasive techniques be used to
promote purchasing and engaging with second-hand clothing on C2C
platforms. Three pilot studies have been conducted to refine the
protocol of the selected methods.
3.1
Method Selection
The initial selection of research methods include focus groups and
creative problem solving (CPS).
3.1.1
Focus Groups. To answer the research wuestion, we ought
to gain insight into the experiences and needs of the user. For this
reason, focus groups are a fruitful method. Focus groups offer the
advantage of interacting simultaneously with multiple participants
and are effective at obtaining user perceptions, satisfaction, and
concerns [22]. Not only are they a practical means of gathering
diverse perspectives, but they also introduce an element of social
interaction [22]. The informal and open atmosphere of a focus
group should encourage an interactive group discussion, aiding
data collection.
In essence, the current study assumes the role of a user research
initiative focused on (potential) users of online platforms dedicated
to purchasing second-hand clothing. In this context, employing
focus groups should be an effective method to learn from (potential)
users to understand why, how and when they would engage with
such a platform. Here the assumption is made that everyone has at
least some experience buying clothing, and most people are aware
of the environmental and ethical concerns.

tional research methods and proposes a solution to the problem.

employed to generate and analyze novel solutions [25]. Creative

cooperative in nature and best done in team efforts [15, 19]. With
various techniques like design thinking, brainstorming, and collab-
orative ideation, we can develop appealing and practical solutions
fitting the target group and resonate with consumers [19].
The participants will be involved in the CPS sessions, ensuring
the CPS outcomes align with the needs of the user and finding

3.2
Participants
A total of nine people participated in our study, of which 3 were
male and 6 female. Participants were recruited through conve-
nience and purposive sampling methods. An open invitation was
sent out to approximately 30 people within social circles, of which
nine responded. Though no specific inclusion criteria were set, the
resulting sample exhibits diversity in backgrounds and affiliation
with the topic. Such diversity fostered an array of perspectives
which stimulates discussion and insights. All participants provided
verbal consent to the recording and processing of their data. On
average, our participants purchased one clothing item every two
weeks to one month on average (excluding garments needing fre-
quent replacement, f.e. socks). More specifics on the profiles of all
participants are displayed in Table 1
The participants were divided into two groups which both under-
went a focus group as well as a creative problem solving session.
Therefore two focusgroups and two CPS sessions were held. One
session of both methods lasted around 1 to 1.5 hours (not including
breaks). This resulted in sufficient data for analysis. Lastly, it was
a deliberate choice to use the same participants for both methods
(opposed to different participants for every separate session), for
several reasons: 1.) convenience, 2.) participants would already be
comfortable with each other and the topic therefore efficiency and
3.) by first critically discussing the topic, participants formed more
familiarity with the topic and identifying particular issues which
could later be applied to the CPS.
3.3
Materials
3.3.1
Focus Groups. For the focus group, we synthesised our own
topic list accompanied by questions. The topics were carefully
selected to suit the research question and reveal participants’ atti-
tudes, behaviors and values towards second-hand clothing. The
topic list consisted of six topics. The first topic explored the partic-
ipants’ shopping habits, revealing 1.) frequency and 2.) where they
would buy their clothing. The second topic dove directly into ethi-
cal and environmental considerations when buying clothing, while
maintaining a broad perspective. The next topic was specifically on
second-hand clothing, inquiring participants’ motivations for (not)
buying second-hand clothing. On a similar topic, the next question
was about comparing the (dis)advantages of new clothing and used
clothing. Fifth, the topic further narrowed down to second-hand
C2C platforms, specifically asking the participants about their ex-
periences on shopping for second-hand clothing online. The focus
group topic list concludes with a comparison of online and in-store
second-hand, together with the inquiry what improvements the
participants would like to see on second-hand C2C platforms. The
full protocol is documented in Appendix A. This protocol denoted
the structure and questions during the discussion. Furthermore, a
recording device and notepad was used to record the data.
3.3.2
CPS. The creative problem solving sessions required sticky
notes and pens. Additionally, two prompts were used to build the
brainstorming exercises upon. The first prompt was "How can we
persuade people to buy more second-hand clothing instead of new?".
This prompt was intentionally broadly oriented, which allows for
interpretation and get participants into a flow. The second prompt
was "How can we persuade people to buy more second-hand clothing
on CMC online platforms, like Vinted". This statement narrowed the
scope down to online contexts only, for more productive responses.
Both prompts directly correspond to our research aims, which
was finding a persuasive technique to expanding engagement with
second-hand C2C clothing platforms.
3.4
Procedure
3.4.1
Focus Groups. At the start of the focus group sessions, all
participants were briefly introduced to the topic and purpose of the
study. They were informed about the structure of the focus group,
expected duration and that the audio will be recorded. Participants
were given the chance to ask any questions before moving on to
the core of the focus group. During the core, one researcher led
the conversation and posed questions to the group in the order as
outlined in the protocol (Q1 - Q6 in Appendix A). The first question
(Q1) served as an easy introductory question and to learn about the
participants clothing shopping habits. They were asked how often
and where they usually buy clothing. While this is not directly
relevant to answering the research question, it does help establish
common ground between the participants and get a better insights
about each person’s shopping behaviour. The following question
(Q2) directed the conversation towards the topic of environment
and ethics. Participants were asked how environmental and ethical
considerations play a role when they buy clothing. Question 3 then
started with a show of hands, asking participants who occasionally
buy second-hand clothing to raise their hand. A show of hands
helped encourage all participants to contribute to the conversation,
and allowed the researcher to pick one or more people to share
why they do or do not buy second-hand. The full group was then
asked to share a time when they chose to buy second-hand clothing
instead of a new item (Q4). A follow-up question was asked to
the participants who answered to elaborate on what influenced
that decision. Finally, the fifth and sixth question inquired the
participants about their experiences buying second hand clothing
online, and what they think are the main differences between
buying online compared to in-store. A follow-up question asked
3

--- Page 4 ---
participants about improvements they would like to see in online
second-hand clothing shopping. This last question acted as a bridge
to the next session: creative problem solving.
3.4.2
CPS. The creative problem solving session started with the
leading researcher explaining CPS as a method and its purpose,
together with an overview of the planned techniques. Relevant con-
cepts such as persuasive techniques were defined. Additionally, an
example of a creative solution was given. Next, participants were
given instructions of the brainstorming exercises. A first round
of brainstorming exercises were done based on the first prompt
(defined in 3.3.2). The participants were instructed to carefully
think about solutions to the prompt, and write them down in short
statements on a sticky note. When the participants were out of
ideas, a role-playing exercise was introduced as a mental break.
After the role-playing exercise, the participants were given a new
yet similar prompt, to which the participants wrote down solutions.
Discussion and elaboration on ideas was encouraged during the
brainstorming sessions. Finally, all solutions were reviewed in a
group discussion. For each solution two points were discussed: 1.)
how feasible is this idea?, and 2.) how will this idea provide a solu-
tion? During the second point, how will this idea provide a solution
opportunity arose to address previously discussed topics, during
the focus group session. Specifically, the participants discussed
how a solution would address the faced challenges or how it would
harness the advantages of second-hand clothing platforms. This
way, the CPS was related back to the focus group, and allowed
for new ideas and insights. Finally, all solutions were ranked on
overall impact on a scale from 1-5. Elaboration of the protocol can
be found in Appendix B.
3.5
Analysis
3.5.1
Focus Groups. Audio recordings of both focus groups were
automatically transcribed into written text. These transcripts were
manually adjusted where needed. Using Microsoft Word, the writ-
ten transcripts were coded according to the open and axial coding
approach of the Straussian Grounded Theory, which allows to
construct new theories from the collected data [23]. Each code was
supported with a quote, demonstrating each code. Either transcript
was initially coded by only one researcher, following a split coding
strategy [20]. After both transcripts were coded, the researchers
exchanged the transcript for validation and to align the codes
used. Any disparities were discussed to consensus, and the codes
were combined into one codebook. This split coding approach
was chosen as it is relatively efficient for small teams, with the
second validation step ensuring the accuracy and reliability of
the codes [20]. During the open coding phase, relevant segments
of the transcript were categorized into codes that captured their
meaning in a short sentence. These codes were derived through
an emergent coding approach. Emergent coding is an inductive
process, wherein the codes emerge from the concepts found within
the collected data, rather than being predetermined by established
theories. The open codes were then categorized into themes that
were discussed during the focus group. For example, ’reasons in
favour of buying second hand’ or ’perceptions of buying second
hand clothing online’. These themes were established a priori dur-
ing the creation of the focus group protocol. After the open coding
phase, common themes and connections were identified between
the codes to create a number of axial codes. As the last step in
Straussian Grounded Theory, two all-encompassing selective codes
unified the axial codes into a core category. Finally, the codes were
visualized into a tabular format. This visualization allows for a
clearer understanding of which codes correspond to each topic, and
distinguishes between negative and positive perceptions.
3.5.2
CPS. The data from the creative problem solving sessions
was recorded on sticky notes. During the group evaluation, the
discussion points were written down on the corresponding sticky
note, together with a rank from 1-5. The next step was to filter and
digitize the sticky notes, which was done by manually entering
solutions and their discussion points into an Excel sheet. Dupli-
cate or obvious incorrect solutions were filtered. When all data
was transferred, each solution was analysed with a SWOT ma-
trix. A SWOT matrix or analysis is known as a strategic planning
tool, commonly used in business contexts [8]. A SWOT analysis
identifies and evaluates the Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities
and Threats [8]. In this case, the strength and weaknesses repre-
sented the clear internal factors that contribute to or challenge
the employment of the solution. The opportunities and threats
represent external factors or situations that either could lead to
positive or negative outcomes of the solution [8]. Previously men-
tioned, each solution received a rank based on overall impact it
would have on the problem. The ranking system ranged from 1-5,
with 1 indicating low impact and 5 indicating high impact. The
initial rankings were subject to review by both researchers and
were adjusted if necessary. Finally, all ideas were categorized based
on Type, which captured which type of solution this was (f.e., a
web-app feature) and Category, grouped solutions based on their
nature (for example; "UX & Convenience").
4
RESULTS
In the present study, we investigated how persuasive strategies
could be applied to promote engagement on second-hand cloth-
ing on consumer-to-consumer (CMC) platforms. For this research
question, we examined the challenges and advantages that users
experience with second-hand clothing (platforms), through focus
group interviews. Later, two creative problem sessions were hosted
to arrive at seven persuasive strategies.
4.1
Focus Groups
A total of 34 open codes and seven axial codes were derived from
the focus groups transcripts. The results are categorized into three
themes that were discussed in the focus groups: comparing second-
hand clothing with new, comparing online with in-store, and improv-
ing C2C platforms. The first two themes are related to the selective
code Considerations when buying clothing, whereas the third theme
belongs to the selective code Desired improvements for C2C plat-
forms. A detailed overview of the focus group results and codes
can be found in Appendix C At the end, we translate these results
into a few main findings that capture the main notions learned
from the participants (see Table 2).

DISCUSSION
In this paper, we investigated consumers’ motivations of buying
second-hand clothing on consumer-to-consumer platforms. Sub-
sequently, we reviewed seven persuasive strategies to combat the
associated challenges of buying second-hand clothing online. First
and foremost, we found that participants were very concerned with
the environmental impact fast fashion has. Secondly, we found
participants believe that second-hand clothing is usually of good
quality, which is an important consideration when buying clothing.
Third, most important factors besides environmental considera-
tions were that ethical (concerns for human rights), materialistic
8

--- Page 9 ---
(price and quality) and social (social influences) in nature. Regard-
ing second-hand clothing online, we found participants valued
shopping experience highly when deciding between buying online
or in-store second-hand clothing. Participants disliked the way
platforms present and recommend clothing items. Finally, partici-
pants felt held back by inconvenience and logistic difficulties of
C2C platforms. According to these findings, we proposed seven
persuasive techniques: pop-up stores for C2C platforms, a button
for ecological impact, brand accounts on C2C platforms, style-
based categorization, AI-powered stylist bot, discussion section
underneath listings and a pick-up parcel service for sellers.
Based on the findings by Jaman et al. (2019), we expected to find
distinct consumer groups, each holding different sets of values.
Rather, people tend hold a mixture of attributes. Specifically ethical,
materialistic, and social motivations are of important considera-
tion when someone decides whether or not to buy second-hand.
These findings mostly align with earlier research by that suggest
that economic, environmental, ethical and hedonic reasons are the
primary drivers of second-hand shopping [5, 6, 9, 10]. The main
distinction lies in how the motivations are categorized and named.
In the current study, environmental and ethical considerations
are treated synonymously, and economic reasons are interpreted
more broadly as ’materialistic’. Although there are variations in
how literature categorizes motivations, they all suggest that con-
sumers likely hold mixed values of both self-interest and ethical
considerations. Our results also underscore this notion.
Additionally, this study revealed that people place a high value
on the perceived shopping experience when deciding between
buying second hand online compared to in-store. If the experience
is negative, this discourages people from buying second-hand. In
that sense, shopping experience can also be seen as a motivation,
closely related with the hedonic reasons defined in earlier studies
[5, 10]. Similarly, existing research confirms that consumer habits
are not easily generalized. For example, there are nuanced differ-
ences in values of people who buy in-store compared to those who
buy online. For this reason, the majority of the proposed features
from CPS are derived from focus group findings associated with
shopping experience (4, 5, and 6 in Table 2). Linking features to
findings 1, 2 and 3 was more challenging, as these were related to
personal values, opinions, or the nature of the clothing itself. For
instance, the belief that second-hand clothing is of high quality
but may sometimes have an unfavorable appearance is an inherent
characteristic of second-hand items and is challenging to influence
using C2C features. Conversely, aspects related to shopping expe-
rience, such as inconveniences or logistic limitations, are directly
tied to C2C platforms and therefore more easily addressed by the
introduction/alteration of features and/or strategy.
Evidently, our participants recognized the environmental urgency
of sustainable shopping behavior. However, almost all participants
revealed they still bought fast-fashion at least once a month. This
inconsistency in values and behavior is contrasting current experi-
mental research on environmentally consistent behavior, which
found that cognitive dissonance can lead to behavior modifications
that align with environmentally friendly practices [1]. At the same
time, other studies fail to find such correlations [21]. A study by
Thøgersen (2004) suggested that in general, people show envi-
ronmentally congruent behavior, but inconsistencies arise when
bounds are faced (f.e., one cannot always choose travelling by train
if there is no other option)[24] . Possibly, quitting consumption of
fast fashion entirely may not be a viable option for many individu-
als, as long as such bounds exist. Our study revealed among other
things that certain barriers to buying second-hand clothing indeed
remain, further emphasizing the necessity for intervention, such
as the proposed strategies in the current paper.
5.1
Limitations and Future Work
Though the study has confirmed several findings and provided
further ground to the research area, several limitations ought to
be mentioned. First, the fact that the selection procedure of the
creative problem solving solutions was done in a manner that was
prone to subjectivity. Though intended to execute the selection in
the most systematic manner we could think of, there is no guar-
antee that these resulted selection is optimal. First, the ranking
attribute of each solution was, though based on a SWOT-analysis,
inherently a subjective measure (additionally, the objectivity of a
SWOT-analysis may just as well be criticized). The same goes for
relation to the findings; no weights were attributed to any find-
ings. To select based on the quantity of relations to theory, ignores
that some findings may be more profound than others. For future
research, it would be of essence to not stop at the documentation
of solutions, but continue and test and implement each solution
so that effectiveness of an intervention is ensured. Otherwise, re-
search to accomplish more rigorous standards for creative problem
solving analysis is highly encouraged.
Furthermore, the qualitative nature of this paper imposed addi-
tional challenges. As mentioned before, qualitative research is
inherently subjective. Focus groups bring the risk of participants
influencing one another due to the complexity of group dynamics.
This in particular made it difficult to minimize bias in the current
study. Initially, we estimated the topic of sustainability and second-
hand clothing not to be sensitive. However, during the interviews
arguments between participants about ethical questions arose, in
which we detected judgement towards each others answers. This
in particular showed that social desirability may have played a
larger role than expected. Though qualitative research offers the
advantage of exploration and insight into elaborate experiences, a
more structured approach will be the next logical step. For future
work, the topic of online C2C behavior may assessed with formal
measures, for example scales assessing environmental awareness
in combination of observational data (f.e., shopping behavior). This
would allow for relevant patterns discoveries, for example whether
environmentally awareness is reflected in behavior.
As mentioned, we did not find conclusive evidence for the exis-
tence of distinct customer groups in this study, as it was not the
primary focus. Exploring this aspect in more detail through future
research endeavors in experimental settings could be an interesting
avenue.

Lastly, the findings presented in this study are difficult to gen-
eralize to large populations, given that the sample in the study
was only nine people. Additionally, the sample mostly consisted
of progressive students who are already involved in topics such
as environmentalism. Though we gained useful insights of our
participants, it is unlikely they are representative of a larger popu-
lation. In future work, it may therefore be particularly interesting
to consider different age groups, social-economic status and/or
education.

CONCLUSION
To conclude, the present study explored persuasive techniques
which promote engagement on second-hand clothing on consumer-
to-consumer platforms. With focus groups and creative problem
solving sessions, a more clear understanding of people’s percep-
tions has been established, providing ground for new solutions
aimed at encouraging to purchase second-hand. Seven persuasive
techniques which were most likely to have a positive impact on
the engagement of second-hand clothing platforms were proposed.
Our study has contributed to existing literature by providing ad-
ditional support that the perception of second-hand clothing has
changed. Second-hand clothing is gaining popularity, as environ-
mental awareness and concerns about the origin of new clothing
increases. Online consumer-to-consumer platforms accommodate
this surge in popularity, as they make both buying and selling
second-hand items more accessible. However, our study also iden-
tified areas for improvement in the online second-hand market,
particularly in the areas of convenience, logistics, and the overall
shopping experience. Without improvements, second-hand will re-
tain its high barriers, making it challenging to compete effectively
with fast fashion. Nonetheless, sustainable fashion continues to be
an increasingly relevant topic, due to the environmental impact of
the current clothing industry [16], and choosing second-hand over
new has been proven to be a sustainable alternative [11]. Quitting
the consumption of fast fashion entirely may not be a viable option
for many, and as this study showed, barriers to buying second-
hand items remain high, even though people well recognise its
importance. This further underscores the necessity for interven-
tions, such as the persuasive techniques proposed in the current
paper.

揭示了国际学生和非国际学生不同的学习重点，强调了
明确的职业发展方向对所有学生的重要性，以及就近入
学和选修课程等特定因素对非国际学生的重要性。此外
，研究还揭示了细微的决策时间线，显示了非国际学生
和国际学生的不同步骤和考虑因素。难以找到入学要求
和选修课信息是共同面临的挑战。研究最后提出了量身
定制的大学网站设计方法，旨在满足学生的不同需求，
改善整体用户体验。参与者的见解提出了潜在的改进措
施，包括移动友好布局和自适应设计。本研究为大学优
化其在线平台提供了宝贵的见解，以便在高等教育的动
态环境中更好地为潜在学生提供支持。

1
导言
未来的学生在选择大学和课程时，会非常依赖大学网站
上的在线信息。决策过程依赖于大学网站上提供的信息
，各院校会在网站上详细介绍其课程、师资、设施和整
体校园生活。大学展示这些相关信息的方式多种多样。
在本研究中，我们旨在了解学生在选择大学专业过程中
的体验。他们认为哪些信息很重要？哪些信息会影响他
们的决定？他们在寻找相关信息时是否遇到困难？为了
回答这些问题，我们将采用两种不同的研究方法，即定
性访谈和在线调查，进行定性研究。这些方法将有助于
对学生的观点进行细致入微的探讨，从而深入了解他们
的决策标准和遇到的挑战
在大学网站上寻找信息。通过对学生浏览这些在线信息
的方式进行定性分析，我们可以为大学网站的设计和优
化决策提供支持。我们希望评估此类网站应重点关注哪
些类型的信息，并就如何展示此类信息以提升整体用户
体验提出建议。通过这项研究，我们希望为大学提供切
实可行的指导，帮助他们在不断变化的高等教育数字化
环境中调整自己的在线内容，使之更好地满足未来学生
的信息需求和偏好。
1.1
问题定义
有几项研究探讨了未来学生的决策过程以及在这一过
程中可能发挥作用的因素。Maniu 及其同事[2] 发现
了影响学生选择大学和课程的不同因素。这些因素包
括院校声誉、专业前景、费用、信息来源、地理位置
、社会生活和推荐。马尼乌提到，许多研究人员发现
了一些共同的因素，但这些因素的重要程度各不相同
。不过，他并没有提到这些因素的重要程度是如何变化
的，也没有提到哪些因素被认为比其他因素更重要。
Pampaloni [4] 的另一项研究关注的是准备申请大学的
学生的决策过程。他们对高中生进行了调查，以确定
对其大学申请决策影响最大的因素。研究发现，互联
网是学生查找大学信息的主要资源。研究指出，大学
认识到了维护更新和组织良好的网站的重要性，但在提
供信息和展示信息方面还有可以改进的地方[3]。最后
，研究还提到，如果不考虑不同受众的目标以及信息
的传播对象，就会对潜在学生的决策过程产生负面影
响。因此，有必要对大学选择过程进行定性分析，以
改进大学网站的设计。

访谈

2.1.1
方法。我们采用半结构式访谈深入挖掘个人经历
。通过这种方法，我们可以探讨主观方面、感受和态度
，同时还可以灵活地就特定的副主题提出后续问题。由

方法面临的第一个挑战是，进行适当的访谈需要时间，
这限制了我们可以纳入样本的参与者人数。为了尽量减

个挑战是，很难设计一个既客观又能保持研究定性性质
的分析管道。

为了对访谈进行分析，我们两人一起进行了访谈。这
样，一个人（"采访者"）可以提问，另一个人（"撰稿
人"）可以对参与者的口语进行配对。通过这种结构

稿人的转述进行交叉核对，以确保文本的有效性。
调查获得的数据可以自动导出到 Excel 文件表中，
因为谷歌文档软件可以做到这一点。为了保持不同方
法之间数据分析的一致性，访谈数据的汇总表与调查
数据类似。
随后，采用基础理论分析方法对获得的数据进行分析
。这种系统方法常用于定性研究，强调了本研究的探索
性质。基础理论旨在直接从数据中产生理论，让概念和
类别有机地出现，而不是预先确定[1]。分析包括对数据
进行系统的编码和分类，以确定模式、主题和关系。这
种方法具有灵活性，能对数据做出反应，并能对定性互
动观点中参与者的生活经验提出细致入微的见解。因此
，我们根据从数据中发现的不同模式尝试了颜色编码。
在这个反复的过程中，我们发现，根据国籍进行分类被
证明是相当有洞察力的。
根据已确定的模式，创建了多种可视化方法，以提供
清晰度、加强理解、突出趋势和关系，并促进这些不同
趋势之间的比较。

程的步骤也有所不同。
除了所有学生都可以利用的在线资源（如朋友、个人
经历、语言知识等）外，非国际学生还可以利用多种资
源来了解大学课程。由于他们往往掌握更多关于大学地
点、学费、教育方式和质量的信息，因此他们的决策过
程似乎涉及的步骤较少。他们通常会先考察住家附近的
院校。然后，他们会寻找自己感兴趣领域的具体专业。
他们还有一个独特的选择，那就是参加学校开放日，在
那里他们可以见到在校学生和教师，体验他们今后几年
的学习环境。
另一方面，国际学生通常会考虑更多的选择，但拥
有的学校资源较少，因此决策过程较长。首先，他们要
研究教育体制和语言要求。一旦选定了一些选择，他
们就会寻找相关项目并评估自己的资格。随后，他们会
了解学费信息，并与其他大学及其预算进行比较。这
一过程的最后一步是阅读有关课程的详细信息，如课

著差异。有两个方面是参与者最难找到的信息。首先，
入学要求被认为是一个难点，参与者表示需要更明确、
更详细的标准。其次，可选选修课的模糊性也是另一个
令人担忧的问题，这表明与会者希望对课程结构的概述
更加透明。与会者还提到，很难找到关于职业前景、经
济支持/学费以及所学技能的相关信息。
本研究获得的启示对大学网站的设计和信息展示具
有重要意义。针对国际学生和非国际学生的不同需求
定制内容，对于提升整体用户体验至关重要。鉴于非
留学生和留学生在决策过程中涉及不同的步骤和因素，
鼓励大学在设计其支持机制时注重定制化。对于非国
际学生而言，这可能涉及到强调易于获取的本地信息
来源，以及促进个性化体验，如学校开放日，以满足
他们的特定需求。与此相反，国际学生可能会从综合性
在线资源中获益匪浅，这些资源专门针对语言要求和
学费问题，提供详细的课程信息，并根据他们独特的
决策过程提供有关学生生活的见解。
通过承认这些差异并相应地调整方法，大学可以积
极营造一个更具包容性和用户友好型的环境，最终帮
助未来的学生就其学术历程做出明智的决定。
总之，本研究为目前正在进行的关于优化大学网站设
计和内容的研究做出了贡献，以适应高等教育数字化环
境下未来学生不断变化的偏好和信息需求。
4.1
局限性
这项研究虽然很有见地，但也遇到了一些必须承认的局
限性。首先，参与者的人口结构存在明显的不平衡，
很大一部分调查对象（50%）是荷兰人。样本缺乏多样
性可能会限制研究结果的普遍性。更多样化的数据集可
能会得出适用性更广、支持性更强的结论。此外，在荷
兰大学就读的参与者占多数（调查中占 71%，访谈中占 
86%），这表明他们与大学网站的互动可能存在偏差。
这些参与者可能更多地接触荷兰大学网站，这可能会影
响他们的看法和批评，可能是由于熟悉或
重点关注这些特定网站的局限性。
相当多的参与者也是乌特勒支大学人机交互专业的学
生。这一专业意味着他们可能比普通人更熟悉用户界面
的理解和交互。他们在网站设计和可用性方面的知识和
经验可能会导致他们的回答出现偏差，尤其是在访谈中
，他们可能会更有效地表达自己对网站的看法。这种情
况可能会对他们的观点产生偏见，而他们的观点可能
并不代表普通网站用户的观点。
此外，时间限制以及调查和访谈方法的参与者人数有
限也影响了我们研究结果的稳健性。如果样本规模更大
、种类更多，就可以进行更全面的数据分析，得出更有
力的结论，从而使研究受益匪浅。
最后，缺乏有关网站实际使用情况的数据是一个
明显的局限。访问大学网站的用户多种多样，如果没
有关于这些网站使用情况的直接数据，我们对用户互
动和感知的理解仍然是不完整的。这一数据缺口限制
了我们充分理解不同用户群体如何与大学网站互动
和如何看待大学网站的能力。

Navigating the Digital Campus: Understanding the
Human-Computer Interaction in University Selection
Processes
Abstract
This research explores the decision-making processes of
prospective university students by employing a dual-method
approach, combining qualitative interviews and online sur-
veys. The study focuses on revealing patterns, timelines,
and challenges encountered by prospective students when
selecting university programs. Analysis reveals distinct in-
formation priorities for international and non-international
students, emphasizing the importance of clear career paths
for all students and specific factors, such as proximity and
elective courses, for non-international students. Addition-
ally, the study uncovers a nuanced decision-making time-
line, demonstrating varied steps and considerations for non-
international and international students. Difficulties in find-
ing entry requirements and elective information emerge as
common challenges. The research concludes by proposing
tailored approaches for university website design, aiming to
address the diverse needs of students and improve overall
user experience. Insights from participants suggest poten-
tial enhancements, including mobile-friendly layouts and
adaptive designs. This study contributes valuable insights to
inform universities on optimizing their online platforms to
better support prospective students in the dynamic landscape
of higher education.
Keywords: University websites, University selection, Deci-
sion making process, Website organisation
1
Introduction
When prospective students are choosing a university and
a course to study, they’ll greatly rely on the information
that is available online on university websites. The decision-
making process is reliant on the availability of information
on university websites, where institutions present details
about their programs, faculty, facilities, and overall campus
life. There’s a significant variety in the way in which univer-
sities have displayed this relevant information. In this study,
we aim to uncover students’ experiences with the process of
choosing a university program. What information do they
find important? What information influences their decision?
Do they experience any difficulties with finding relevant
information? In order to answer such questions, we will con-
duct qualitative research existing of two different research
methods, namely qualitative interviews and an online survey.
These methods will facilitate a nuanced exploration of stu-
dents’ perspectives, allowing for in-depth insights into their
decision-making criteria and the challenges they encounter
when seeking information on university websites. By get-
ting a qualitative insight into the way that students navigate
this online information, we can support university website
design and optimization decisions. We hope to assess which
types of information are to be focused on such websites
and to provide recommendations on how such information
should be presented to enhance the overall user experience.
Through this research, we aspire to offer practical guidance
to universities, helping them tailor their online content to
better align with the informational needs and preferences of
prospective students in the ever-evolving digital landscape
of higher education.

There are several studies that have explored the decision-
making process that prospective students go through and
the factors that might play a role in this process. Maniu
and colleagues [2] have identified different factors that in-
fluence students’ decisions regarding university and course
selection. These factors include institutional reputation, ca-
reer prospects, costs, information sources, location, social
life, and recommendations. Maniu mentions that many re-
searchers have uncovered common factors, but that the de-
gree of importance of these factors varies. However, it’s not
mentioned in what ways the degree of importance varies or
which factors are considered to be more important than oth-
ers. Another study by Pampaloni [4] focuses on the decision-
making process of students preparing to apply to college.
High school students were surveyed to identify the factors
most influential to their college application decision-making.
The research found that the internet is a primary resource
for students to find information about universities. It’s noted
that universities recognize the importance of maintaining an
updated and well-organized website, but that there are areas
in which they can improve the provided information and
how it’s presented [3]. Lastly, it’s mentioned that failing to
consider differing audience goals and to whom the message
is directed can negatively impact the decision-making pro-
cess of prospective students. Therefore, a qualitative insight
into this university selection process is a necessity in order
to improve the design of university websites.

Study Design
To achieve our research objectives, we have carefully se-
lected two qualitative research methods that align with the
exploratory nature of our study: Interviews and Online Sur-
veys.
2.1
Interviews
In this study, seven participants were engaged in semi-structured
interviews, each lasting approximately twenty minutes, de-
signed to elicit responses seamlessly aligned with the natural
progression of their thoughts, without encountering difficul-
ties in participants’ comprehension or irrelevant disclosures.
2.1.1
Method. We employ semi-structured interviews to
dig deeply into individual experiences. This method allows
us to explore subjective aspects, feelings, and attitudes, while
also providing the flexibility to ask follow-up questions on
specific subtopics. The absence of a group dynamic reduces
social desirability bias. The first challenge posed by this
method is that conducting a proper interview takes time and
this limits the number of participants that we can include
in our sample. In order to minimize the bias caused by the
small sample size, we aim to interview participants with
varying backgrounds, gender, and studies. Another challenge
that comes with interviews is that it is difficult to design an
analysis pipeline that is objective and still preserves the
qualitative nature of the research.
2.1.2
Protocol. The interview questions can be found in
appendix (A). The subsequent protocol was followed:
1. Introduction of the interviewer and the study
In this section the interviewer starts by introducing them-
selves and explains the purpose and the goals of the study.
Additionally, the interviewer will ask for consent of the par-
ticipant for conducting an interview and using the data on
their answers for qualitative research. This consent form can
be found in appendix (D) The interviewer will also let them
know that they can terminate the interview at any time if
they would like to.
2. Introduction of the participant
In this section some relevant background information on the
participant is collected. This includes questions on whether
they are currently studying and what course they study/studied
(Q1), what university they go/went to (Q2), and when they
started university (Q3). The aim of this section is to con-
textualize their experiences and preferences in navigating
online information related to university studies. With this
context we might be able to uncover trends in experiences
for specific demographics.
3. Core of the interview
In this section the questions relating to our research ques-
tions are asked. Each question is asked with a specific pur-
pose. Q1 aims to understand the initial steps participants
take when beginning their search for universities. It helps
uncover the starting points and key considerations in their
decision-making process. For students who attended mulit-
ple university programes (e.g. a Bachelor’s and a Master’s
programs) the questions are phrased clearly to avoid con-
fusion about which university selection process to present:
’Please answer according to your most recent study. If you re-
member this experience better for any prior studies that you
may have done, or if you find that you have relevant expe-
riences with the process of choosing a previous study and/or
university, you are also allowed to answer according to that
study.’
Q2 and Q3 help in understanding the specific criteria and
information that participants consider important in their
decision-making process and shed light on the factors influ-
encing their choices. By the follow up questions we might
be able to identify challenges in information retrieval which
helps pinpoint areas where university websites may lack clar-
ity or depth, guiding improvements for a more user-friendly
experience. Q4 helps to understand participants’ application
strategies and outcomes. It also helps to reveal the dynamics
of their decision-making process, particularly in cases where
they applied to multiple institutions. Q5 assesses the accu-
racy and reliability of information provided on university
websites. Q6 could offer valuable insights into potential areas
for improvement in the information available. Subsequently,
a list of specific types of information that one might have
seen on university websites is displayed to the participants
to avoid the repetativeness of the questions (Q7). The partic-
ipant is asked to optionally sort them, share their opinion
about how important they think it is to display them, and
how much they matter in terms of making an informed deci-
sion. By this structure, we aim to provide an overview of the
types of information while minimizing potential cognitive
load and maintaining participant engagement. The responses
to these elements collectively contribute to a nuanced un-
derstanding of how different factors shape participants’ in-
formed decision-making processes.
4. Ending the interview
Here the participant is asked if there is anything they would
like to add. This way we might be able to uncover things that
prospective students deem important that were excluded in
the interview. Lastly, the participant is thanked for their
participation and it is once again explained that the data
they provided will be used in our research anonymously for
qualitative analysis.

2.2
Survey
In this research, a well-structured survey was administered
to 14 participants, showcasing strengths that greatly en-
hanced the integrity of our study. The survey’s design not
only enabled precise data collection but also incorporated
free-text sections that elicited unexpected yet valuable in-
sights, thereby broadening our data spectrum and ensuring
participant engagement and response quality.
2.2.1
Method. Thousands of students are utilizing univer-
sity websites to help them decide which university program
to choose. Recognizing the need to reach a large and diverse
user group, we utilize surveys to uncover insights that partic-
ipants might be hesitant to share in face-to-face interactions.
The online format allows respondents to contemplate their
responses at their own pace, promoting thoughtful feedback.
While there are many advantages of this research method,
there are a few limitations that we have to pay attention to,
when designing the survey. Firstly, there is no opportunity
for clarification when the participants fill out the question
form. The questions therefore have to be as self-explanatory
and clear as possible. Secondly, the absence of visible body-
language. When analyzing the data from the survey, we have
to consider that the non-verbal information is missing from
our dataset. Using interviews in addition to the surveys is
one way of reducing this uncertainty.
2.2.2
Protocol. The questions can be found in appendix
(B).
1. Introduction of the study Before starting the survey,
a debriefing form is presented which includes a summary
of the study, why and how this survey data is collected, a
short explanation on how to answer the questions, how long
the survey will approximately take, and lastly we thank the
participant for taking part in our study. This debrief can be
found in appendix C. (C)
2. Introduction of the participant
This section is very similar to the second part of the initial
interview protocol. Similarly, relevant background informa-
tion on the participant is collected. Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4 are all
part of this introduction, with the aim of uncovering trends
in experiences for specific demographics.
3. Core of the survey
Q5 until Q9 relate to answering our research questions. These
questions are very similar to Q2, Q3, Q6, and Q7 from the
interviews. Asking similar questions in the survey is a valu-
able addition to the interviews. While interviews provide in-
depth insights from a smaller group of participants, surveys
enable you to reach a larger and more diverse sample. This
broader representation can help identify common trends and
patterns that might not be evident in a smaller set of quali-
tative interviews. The survey questions were meticulously
crafted to ensure clarity in the level of detail required for
each response. Instead of limiting answers to binary choices
like ’yes’ or ’no’, we allowed free-text responses, providing
participants the flexibility to elaborate on their experiences
as they deemed necessary.
4. Ending the survey
In this section the participant is asked if there is anything else
they would like to share on the topic (Q10), which could help
us in discovering new angles. If the participant would like to
read our research paper once it is finished, they are asked to
provide their email address so that the paper including the
study results can be sent through (Q11).
2.2.3
Participants. All people filling out the survey were
university students or alumni who started their most recent
university program after 2010, just like the interview par-
ticipants. A similar sample group for the two methods is
ideal for comparing the results. The participants were re-
cruited online through social media platforms (e.g. Facebook,
Whatsapp).
2.3
Synergy of Chosen Methods
This study employs a complementary approach by integrat-
ing both interviews and surveys, focusing on the unique
strengths of each method to address the complexity of uni-
versity website navigation and decision-making processes.
Interviews offer in-depth insights and nuanced understand-
ings of individual experiences, allowing us to explore the sub-
jective aspects of human-computer interactions that surveys
alone might overlook. On the other hand, surveys provide a
broader perspective, quantifying trends and patterns across
a larger sample of prospective university students. This data

complements the qualitative richness of the interviews, en-
suring a more comprehensive analysis. Furthermore, the sur-
veys help in validating and generalizing the findings from the
interviews, while the interviews offer detailed explanations
and contextual depth to the patterns observed in survey re-
sponses. Together, these methods create a robust framework
for understanding and improving the design of university
websites to better support student decision-making.
2.4
Analysis
In order to analyse the interviews we conducted them with
the two of us. This way one person could be asking the
questions, ’the interviewer’, and the other person could para-
phrase the spoken words of the participants, ’the writer’.
By this structure, it’s ensured that the flow of the interview
is not disrupted by the interviewer having to hold the con-
versation and make certain that all important aspects are
written down at the same time. Another advantage of two re-
searchers being present is that the paraphrasing of the writer
could immediately be cross-checked by the interviewer to
guarantee the validity of the text.
The data obtained from the survey could automatically be
exported into an Excel file table as the Google Documents
software enables this. To keep the data analysis between
methods consistent, the data from the interviews is sum-
marised in a table similar to the survey data.
Subsequently to analyse the obtained data, the method of
grounded theory analysis is used. This systematic method is
commonly used in qualitative research and emphasizes the
exploratory nature of this study. Grounded theory aims to
generate theory directly from the data, allowing concepts
and categories to emerge organically rather than being pre-
determined [1]. The analysis involves systematically coding
and categorizing the data to identify patterns, themes, and
relationships. This method allows for flexibility, responsive-
ness to the data, and the development of nuanced insights
into the lived experiences of participants in qualitative inter-
views. Thus, we experimented with color coding according
to different patterns that were uncovered through the data.
Based on this iterative process, it was found that categorizing
according to nationality proved to be rather insightful.
In light of the identified patterns, multiple visualizations
are created to provide clarity, enhance understanding, high-
light trends and relationships, and to facilitate comparison
between these different trends.

Study Results and Discussion
Initially we explored the pre-analyzed dataset without pre-
conceived notions, to uncover patterns within the interview
and survey data. Our analysis led us to identify distinct dif-
ferences in the decision-making processes and factors in-
fluencing international and non-international students. We
defined non-international students as those who chose their
university program in their home country, and international
as those who attend a university program outside of their
home country.
Secondly, our research focused on identifying potential
challenges and roadblocks that prospective students encounter
when choosing specific university courses in their preferred
fields. This exploration aims to inform future designs, en-
abling the application of creative problem-solving or design-
thinking approaches to enhance the student experience.
While the first two subsections are based on patterns and
trends, the third section of our findings shifts focus on pre-
senting unique, standalone insights. These insights, though
not forming repetitive patterns, emerged as relevant and
thought-provoking contributions to our understanding of
students’ interactions with university websites.
3.1

3.1.1
What are applicants looking for? In exploring
what applicants seek on university websites, our study, through
both interviews and surveys, asked participants to share their
views on the types of information typically presented on

mentioned by students in other contexts.
Common priorities for both non-international and
international students:
Students universally value information about career op-

students showed a preference for elective courses to cus-

students trusted the experts to design their curriculum and

Timeline of decision-making process. As men-
tioned above, non-international and international students
seem to have different deciding factors in the university se-
lection process. Our dataset suggests that this is not the only
difference between them. The steps of the decision-making
process also differ.
Non-international students have multiple resources to in-
form themselves about university programs, in addition to
the online resources available to all students (such as friends,
personal experiences, language knowledge, etc.). Since they
tend to have more information about locations, tuition fees,
education styles, and quality of universities, their decision-
making process appears to involve fewer steps. They usually
start by exploring institutions near their homes. Then, they
seek a specific program in their field of interest. Another
unique option available to them is participating in offline
open days, where they can meet current students, and teach-
ers, and experience the environment where they will spend
their subsequent years.
On the other hand, international students typically con-
sider more options but have fewer offline resources, making
the decision-making process longer. First, they research the
education systems and language requirements. Once they
have selected some options, they look for relevant programs
and assess their eligibility. Subsequently, they seek informa-
tion about tuition fees and compare them with other uni-
versities and their budgets. The final step in the process is
to read about the details of the programs, such as course
descriptions or information about student life.

seemed to be no significant difference in the difficulties that

students. That being said, 8 out of the 14 survey responses
did not experience any difficulties. On the other hand, all
interviewees explained to have experienced some difficulties.
An explanation for these contrasting findings could be that
the interviewees felt more inclined to provide a thorough
answer because of the face-to-face interaction. Whereas the
online survey respondents might not have felt this inclina-
tion in the same way.
Between both the interviews and the surveys, three par-
ticipants mentioned that they had experienced difficulties
with finding relevant information on the entry requirements.
The issue here is not in the absence of this information, the
entry requirements do seem to be available. However, the
information seems to be incomplete. One participant men-
tioned that it was too vague, they would have prefered it if
the requirements would have been more clear cut (e.g. "if
you have done A, you can get in. If you have done B, you
cannot").
Another type of information that three participants strug-
gled to find, is information on the electives that one is al-
lowed to take. An overview of the structure of the course
and more elaborate information on which electives you can
and cannot choose is something that the participants would
want to be clearer. One participant explained: "The gist of
the study might sound good but you do have to do the work, so
know what you can expect in the specific courses".
Information on career prospects, financial support / tu-
ition fees, and skills one would learn was also mentioned by
participants as difficult to find relevant information on.

Neither group found university rankings to be important,

also mentioned by participants as difficult to find relevant

The insights gained from this study hold implications
for university website design and information presentation.
Tailoring content to address the diverse needs of both in-
ternational and non-international students is crucial for en-
hancing the overall user experience. In light of the varied
steps and factors involved in the decision-making processes
of non-international and international students, universities
are encouraged to design their support mechanisms with
a focus on customization. For non-international students,

offline open days, to cater to their specific needs. In contrast,
international students may benefit significantly from the pro-

tent to align with the evolving preferences and informational
needs of prospective students in the digital landscape of
higher education.
4.1
Limitations
This research, while insightful, encounters several limita-
tions that must be acknowledged. Firstly, there is a notable
imbalance in the participant demographic, with a substantial
portion of survey respondents (50%) being Dutch. This lack
of diversity in the sample potentially limits the generalizabil-
ity of the findings. A more diverse dataset would likely yield
conclusions with broader applicability and stronger support.
Additionally, the predominance of participants attending
Dutch universities (71% in surveys and 86% in interviews)
suggests a possible bias in their interaction with univer-
sity websites. These participants are likely more exposed
to Dutch university websites, which might influence their
perceptions and critiques, either through familiarity or a
focus on the limitations of these specific websites.
A significant number of participants are also students
of Human-Computer Interaction at Utrecht University. This
specialization means they are presumably more familiar with
understanding and interacting with user interfaces than the
average person. Their prior knowledge and experience with
website design and usability could result in biased responses,
particularly in interviews where they might articulate their
opinions on websites more effectively. This situation could
introduce a bias towards their viewpoints, which may not
be representative of the average website user.
Furthermore, time constraints and the limited number of
participants for both survey and interview methods affect
the robustness of our findings. The research would benefit
from a larger and more varied sample size, allowing for more
comprehensive data analysis and stronger conclusions.
Lastly, the absence of data on actual website usage is a
notable limitation. University websites are accessed by a
diverse range of users, and without direct data on how these
websites are used, our understanding of user interactions
and perceptions remains incomplete. This gap in data limits
our ability to fully comprehend how different user groups
interact with and perceive university websites.

Some of these insights focused on the structure and func-
tionality of the websites. Among others they mention the
possibility of creating a mobile layout for the main pages

As some of these good insights can lead to simple practical

be worthy of future research.

Simple random sample
Get a list (“sampling frame”)
This is hard; must not systematically exclude anyone

Generate random numbers

Select persons from list based on these numbers

--- Slide 7 ---
Systematic random sampling
Select a random number, say k (“sampling interval”)

Get a list of people, or observe a flow of people 
e.g. pedestrians passing by, or customers in line for coffee

Select every kth person 
make sure there is no systematic variation in the list, e.g. if every second person is a parent
--- Slide 8 ---
Stratified sampling
Separate your population into groups (“strata”)
Must know easily what those groups are
Can be based on survey (e.g. main purpose of travel)
Simple or systematic random sample per group
Number selected per strata can reflect proportions

--- Slide 9 ---
Cluster sampling
Population divided into subgroups (“clusters”)
E.g. districts, divisions of a company
Simple or systematic random sample per cluster

--- Slide 10 ---
Multi-stage cluster sampling

Get a list of “custers”
E.g. branches of a company

Randomly sample clusters from that list

Randomly sample people within those clusters

This can also be done in more stages
Random clusters, random sub-clusters of those clusters, random sample of people of those sub-clusters
